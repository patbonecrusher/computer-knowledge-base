# Abner Ebenezer Wolcott

## Personal Information
- **Birth**: c. 1747
- **Birthplace**: Likely Connecticut or Vermont
- **Death**: August 1, 1833
- **Death Location**: Sorel, Quebec, Canada
- **Burial**: Christ Church Anglican Cemetery, Sorel, Quebec
- **Age at Death**: 86 years
- **Father**: John Wolcott V(2) (c.1730-after 1778)
- **Occupations**: Farmer, Teamster (hauled provisions)
- **Loyalty**: United Empire Loyalist

### Marriages
1. **Catherine Griffen** - married January 8, 1772 in Oxford, Connecticut
2. **Dorothy Redman** (widow) - married 1821

### Children
At least 10 children (from first marriage):
- **John Wolcott** (b. 1772 Oxford CT, d. 1847 Verchères, Quebec)
- **Louis Abner Wolcott** (b. 1774 New Haven VT, d. 1853 St. Damase, Quebec)
- **Solomon Wolcott** (b. 1778 New Haven VT, d. 1815)
- **Catherine Wolcott**
- **Marie Wolcott** (1788-1867) - married Pierre Dansereau
- And at least 5 other children

## Historical Status
- **United Empire Loyalist**: Officially recognized as UEL for loyalty to the British Crown during the American Revolution
- **Refugee**: Fled to Canada during Revolutionary War
- **Compensation Recipient**: Received British government compensation for losses in 1783

## Early Life in New England

### Family Background

Abner was born around 1747 into the Wolcott family, an established English colonial family in New England. The Wolcott family had been in America for over a century:
- **John Wolcott** (before 1599-1638) immigrated from Axbridge, Somerset, England to Watertown, Massachusetts in 1634
- The family spread throughout New England over several generations
- By Abner's time, Wolcotts were settled in Connecticut and Vermont

### Connecticut and Vermont (1747-1777)

Abner grew up in the Connecticut/Vermont region during a pivotal time in American history:
- **1772**: Married Catherine Griffen in Oxford, Connecticut (age 25)
- **1772**: Birth of first son John in Oxford, Connecticut
- **1774**: Birth of second son Louis Abner in New Haven, Vermont
- **1775**: Purchased 150 acres of land at New Haven, Vermont
- **1778**: Birth of third son Solomon in New Haven, Vermont

By 1775, Abner was a landowner and farmer with a growing family.

## The American Revolution: Choosing the Crown

### Loyalist Commitment (1765-1777)

When the American Revolution began, Abner chose to support the British Crown rather than the rebel cause. From 1765-1798, he was an active American Loyalist.

**Why did he choose loyalty to Britain?**
- Many reasons drove Loyalist sympathies: economic ties to Britain, religious beliefs, fear of mob rule, genuine loyalty to the Crown
- Vermont was particularly divided, with both Patriot and Loyalist factions
- The choice would cost Abner everything

### Hauling Provisions for the British Army

Abner worked as a teamster, hauling provisions and supplies for the British army in the northern campaigns. This was dangerous work:
- Patriots considered this treason
- Supply routes were targets for rebel forces
- Teamsters could be captured or killed

### Capture (1777)

In **1777**, Abner was **taken prisoner** by American forces. This was likely after the **Battle of Saratoga** (September-October 1777), one of the turning points of the Revolutionary War where British General John Burgoyne surrendered to American forces.

The timing is significant:
- The British suffered a devastating defeat at Saratoga
- Thousands of British soldiers and Loyalist civilians were captured
- Many Loyalists faced harsh treatment from Patriots
- Abner's property and family were now at risk

## Escape to Canada (1778)

### The Flight North

In **1778**, Abner **escaped from captivity** and fled north to Canada. This was an arduous and dangerous journey:
- Crossing enemy territory to reach British-controlled Canada
- Traveling through wilderness in winter
- Leaving behind property, possessions, and initially, family

### Refugee Camp at Sorel

Abner arrived at the **British military post and refugee camp at Sorel, Quebec**, where his wife Catherine eventually joined him with their young children.

**Sorel:**
- Located at the confluence of the Richelieu and St. Lawrence Rivers
- Strategic British military post
- Major refugee camp for Loyalists fleeing the American colonies
- Hundreds of displaced Loyalist families lived in difficult conditions

The seigneurie of Sorel had been purchased by the British Crown during the American Revolution for military-strategic reasons, and Governor Frederick Haldimand founded a town there to accommodate Loyalist refugees.

**The Refugee Experience:**
- Makeshift housing and poverty
- Separation from extended family
- Loss of property and livelihood
- Uncertainty about the future
- Dependence on British military support

### British Compensation (1783)

After the **Treaty of Paris (1783)** ended the Revolutionary War and American independence was recognized, the British government provided compensation to Loyalists who had lost property.

In **1783**, Abner received **compensation from the British government** for his losses in Vermont. This was part of a larger program to compensate Loyalists who had remained faithful to the Crown.

## Settlement in Quebec (1783-1833)

### Settlement in Sorel Region

After receiving compensation, Abner settled his family in the Sorel area. Land survey records ("Procès-verbal de bornage") from the îles Bouchard in the St. Lawrence River mention Abner Woolcott, and his children were baptized in Catholic churches at St. Sulpice and Verchères, towns on opposite sides of the river.

**Life in the Sorel Region:**
- Farming and establishing a new homestead
- Living among French-speaking Catholic neighbors
- Integration into Quebec society
- Raising children in a bicultural environment

### Religious Complexity

The Wolcott family's religious life in Quebec was complex:

**Catholic Environment:**
- Abner's children were **baptized in Catholic churches**
- Baptisms took place in **St. Sulpice** or **Verchères** (towns on opposite shores of the river from the island)
- In **1798**, children John, Louis Abner, Solomon, and Catherine **formally renounced their Protestant faith and joined the Catholic Church**

**But Abner and Catherine remained Protestant:**
- Abner and his wife Catherine were **buried in a Protestant cemetery** (Christ Church Anglican, Sorel)
- This suggests they never converted, despite their children's conversion

This religious adaptation was practical:
- Quebec was overwhelmingly Catholic
- Marrying and integrating into French-Canadian society required Catholic affiliation
- Many Loyalist families made similar accommodations

### Return to Vermont (c. 1800)

Around **1800**, Abner and his son John returned to **Vergennes, Vermont**. The reasons for this brief return are unknown:
- Perhaps to reclaim property?
- To check on family or business?
- To explore whether they could resettle in the new United States?

Whatever the reason, they did not stay long.

### Oath of Allegiance to the Crown (1803)

Soon after returning from Vermont, Abner and his family took the **Oath of Allegiance to the British Crown in 1803**. This formalized their status as British subjects in Lower Canada and likely confirmed their right to remain on their land.

### Later Life (1803-1833)

Abner lived the remainder of his life in the Sorel region:
- Farming in the area
- Watching his children marry and have families
- Integrating into the French-Canadian community
- Living as a quiet British subject

**Second Marriage (1821):**
- At age 74, Abner married **Dorothy Redman**, a widow
- His first wife Catherine had presumably died
- This late-life marriage suggests companionship in old age

### Will and Final Years (1825-1833)

**Will of 1825:**
- Dated 1825 (age 78)
- Left estate to wife Dolly (Dorothy)
- After her death, estate to be divided among his 10 children
- This will later caused disputes among heirs

**Death (August 1, 1833):**
- Died at Sorel, Quebec at age 86
- Buried at Christ Church Anglican Cemetery, Sorel
- Survived by widow Dorothy and numerous children and grandchildren

**Estate Dispute:**
- His children and widow disputed the will
- The details of this dispute are not fully documented
- Common in cases of second marriages with children from first marriage

## The Cost of Loyalty

### What Abner Lost

By choosing loyalty to the British Crown, Abner Wolcott lost:
- **150 acres of land** in New Haven, Vermont (purchased 1775)
- **Property and possessions** left behind when fleeing
- **Established life** in New England
- **Extended family** who may have remained in the United States
- **Economic security** built over years of work

### What He Gained

By remaining loyal and fleeing to Canada, Abner gained:
- **Safety** for himself and his family
- **British compensation** for losses
- **New land** in Quebec
- **Integration** into French-Canadian society
- **Legacy** as a United Empire Loyalist
- **British subject status** in a land that remained loyal to the Crown

## Legacy and Descendants

### United Empire Loyalist Status

Abner Wolcott is recognized as a **United Empire Loyalist**, a designation of honor in Canada for those who remained loyal to the British Crown during the American Revolution. His descendants can claim UEL heritage.

### Quebec Descendants

Abner's descendants became part of the fabric of Quebec society:
- Many married into French-Canadian families
- Converted to Catholicism
- Adopted French-Canadian customs and language
- Some maintained the Wolcott/Wilcott/Woolcott surname, others francicized it

**Notable Descendant Line:**
- Marie Wolcott (1788-1867) married Pierre Dansereau
- Their daughter Julie Dansereau (1828-1894) married Joseph Tanguay
- This line continued through generations in Quebec

### Surname Variations

The Wolcott surname appears in many variations in Quebec records:
- **Wolcott** (original English spelling)
- **Wilcott** (common variation)
- **Woolcott** (another English variation)
- **Woolcott dit Wilcott** (French-Canadian "dit" name)

Most descendants in early Quebec records used "Wilcott"; later generations returned to "Wolcott."

## Historical Context

### The Loyalist Experience in Canada

Abner's story was shared by thousands of Loyalists:

**Numbers:**
- Approximately 40,000-50,000 Loyalists fled to Canada during and after the Revolution
- About 6,000-7,000 settled in Quebec
- The rest settled in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Ontario

**Settlement Patterns:**
- Loyalists entered Quebec via Lake Champlain and the Richelieu River
- Refugee camps at St. Johns, Three Rivers, and Sorel
- Later settlements along the St. Lawrence River and Eastern Townships
- Some received land grants; others purchased land

**Integration:**
- English-speaking Protestant Loyalists in overwhelmingly French Catholic Quebec
- Many adapted by learning French and accommodating Catholic practices
- Created English enclaves in some areas (Eastern Townships)
- Contributed to Quebec's linguistic and religious diversity

### The Battle of Saratoga (1777)

The Battle of Saratoga (September 19 - October 7, 1777) was the context for Abner's capture:

**The Campaign:**
- British General John Burgoyne led forces south from Canada
- Goal: split New England from other colonies
- American forces under Horatio Gates and Benedict Arnold opposed him

**The Outcome:**
- Devastating British defeat
- Burgoyne surrendered October 17, 1777
- Turning point of the war (convinced France to support Americans)
- Thousands of British soldiers and Loyalist civilians captured

Abner was "with the British" after Saratoga, likely captured in the aftermath.

### Sorel as a Loyalist Refuge

**Governor Frederick Haldimand's Vision:**
- Purchased the seigneurie of Sorel for strategic reasons
- Established town to accommodate Loyalist refugees
- Provided temporary housing and support
- Later facilitated permanent settlement

**Legacy:**
- Sorel became an important Loyalist settlement
- English Protestant community in French Catholic region
- Christ Church Anglican served the English community
- Loyalist descendants remained for generations

## The Wolcott Family Lineage Controversy

### The Missing Link: John Wolcott I to John Wolcott II

Abner Wolcott's lineage traces back to **John Wolcott I** (before 1599-1638) who immigrated from England to Watertown, Massachusetts in 1634. However, there has been historical controversy about the connection between John I and his supposed son **John Wolcott II** (c.1634-1690).

**The Problem:**
- **No clear documentary evidence** originally existed proving John II was the son of John I
- John I died in 1638; John II appeared in Newbury, Massachusetts in the 1650s
- The connection was based on circumstantial evidence:
  - Timing (John II born c.1634-1636, consistent with being John I's son)
  - Location (John II owned land at Watertown, where John I lived)
  - Family connections (John II sold land to Nathaniel Clark, whose father married Sarah Woolcott, John I's daughter)

**The Genealogical Debate:**
- Some genealogists questioned whether the connection was proven
- Others accepted it based on preponderance of circumstantial evidence
- The uncertainty affected all descendants trying to trace their line

### DNA Evidence Confirms the Connection (21st Century)

In the early 2000s, the **Wolcott DNA Project** (begun by Charles W. Wolcott and John B. Wolcott in 2004) sought to resolve genealogical questions using Y-DNA testing.

**The Discovery:**
- Descendants of John Wolcott of Watertown (John I) belong to haplogroup **Y22158**
- This haplogroup branched off from haplogroup Y20600 around **1570 AD**
- No other branches of the Wolcott family belong to Y22158
- All tested descendants of John II also carry Y22158

**Definitive Proof:**
- Gary Lynn Wolcott's positive result for DNA SNP Y22158 provided **definitive proof** that descendants of later Wolcotts (including Silas Wolcott of Pennsylvania and the Brookfield, Massachusetts Wolcott community) descend from John Wolcott I of Watertown
- This confirms that John II was indeed the son of John I

**What This Means:**
- Abner Wolcott's lineage to John Wolcott I is now confirmed by DNA
- The lineage continues back through the Wolcott family to England
- And through the maternal Cornwall line to the Plantagenet royal family
