# Anne Couvent - Gateway to Seven Royal Lineages

## Personal Information

- **Birth**: c. 1601-1604
- **Birthplace**: Estrées (now Cœuvres-et-Valsery), Aisne, Picardy, France
- **Death**: December 25, 1675
- **Death Location**: Quebec, New France
- **Parents**:
  - **Father**: Guillaume Convent dit Estrée
  - **Mother**: Antoinette de Longueval (c.1582-c.1640)
- **Immigration**: 1636 to New France

### Marriages

1. **Philippe Amiot dit Hameau** - married c.1625 in Soissons, France
   - Philippe died before September 1639 in Quebec

2. **Jacques Maheu** - married September 26, 1639 in Quebec
   - Jacques died before 1666

3. **Étienne Blanchon** - married 1666
   - No children from this marriage

### Children

**From First Marriage (Philippe Amiot):**
- Jean Amiot (born c.1625 in France)
- Mathieu Amiot (born c.1626-1628 in France)
- Charles Amiot (baptized August 26, 1636 in Quebec - born in New France)

**From Second Marriage (Jacques Maheu):**
- Marie Madeleine Maheu (died in infancy)
- Jean Maheu

### Nephew
- **Toussaint Ledran** - son of Louis Ledran and Charlotte Couvent (Anne's sister)
  - Also immigrated to New France
  - Shares the same royal lineages through Charlotte Couvent

## Historical Significance

### Gateway Ancestor Status

Anne Couvent is one of the most important **gateway ancestors** for French-Canadians, meaning she provides documented connection to European royalty for thousands of descendants.

She is one of only three women who serve as royal gateways for French-Canadians who settled in New France:
1. **Catherine de Baillon**
2. **Jeanne Le Marchand**
3. **Anne Couvent** (leads back to more recent royals than the other two)

### Research Breakthrough (2007)

Until 2007, Anne Couvent's royal connections were **hidden from history**.

**Groundbreaking Research**:
- Published by **Roland-Yves Gagné** and **Laurent Kokanosky** in 2007
- Established that Anne Couvent descended from royalty through her mother **Antoinette de Longueval**
- Published in: _Mémoires de la Société généalogique canadienne-française_ 58, no. 1, issue 251 (Spring 2007): 17-58
- Title: "Les origines de Philippe Amiot (Hameau), de son épouse Anne Couvent et de leur neveu Toussaint Ledran"

This research overcame significant genealogical challenges to establish this royal gateway.

### The François de Joyeuse Controversy

The royal lineages depend critically on one link: **François de Joyeuse** (c.1520-c.1597).

**The Problem:**
- François is **not mentioned** by **Père Anselme** or **La Chesnaye-Desbois** (the classical French genealogical authorities) among the children of Robert de Joyeuse and Marguerite de Barbançon
- This absence raises questions about whether François was truly their son

**The 1733 Notarial Document:**
- Key evidence comes from a notarial act dated **March 20, 1733**
- Attested by **Jean-Baptiste de Joyeuse** to notary **Pierre Hanonnet** of Verdun
- Claims François de Joyeuse was the **second son** of Robert and Marguerite
- Describes François as the **"black sheep"** of the family

**The "Black Sheep" Story:**
- François rebelled against his parents and married **a woman of very inferior social background** (name unknown—not noble)
- His father Robert, described as "a physically powerful and violent man," was furious
- Robert **disowned François**, leaving him only: **"son nom et son épée"** ("his name and his sword")
- No inheritance, no lands, no titles, no money
- This explains why François doesn't appear in the classical genealogies—he was erased from family records

**The Genealogical Debate:**

**Skeptics** (including Charles Cawley of Foundation for Medieval Genealogy):
- The 1733 document is 164 years after François's death
- It's hearsay from a descendant claiming connection to the disowned line
- No contemporary sources confirm François as Robert's son
- Asking trust in single 18th century document contradicting published genealogies

**Supporters** (Gagné, Kokanosky, John P. DuLong):
- The 1733 document is a **notarial act**—a legal document with formal weight
- The disownment story **explains** François's absence from classical genealogies
- Additional corroborating evidence found in other documents
- DuLong published detailed rebuttal in 2023: *"The Joyeuse Ancestry of the Couvents"*

**Current Status:**
- Most French-Canadian genealogists **accept** the connection
- Some medieval genealogists remain **skeptical**
- The royal lineages are generally recognized—but with acknowledgment of this uncertain link
- Honest genealogists note: this connection has some uncertainty but is based on best available evidence

## The Seven Royal Lineages

Anne Couvent descends from **seven different royal houses of Europe**:

### 1. France: Louis VIII (1187-1226)
**Louis VIII "the Lion", King of France**
- Reigned 1223-1226
- Son of Philip II Augustus
- Father of Saint Louis IX

### 2. England: Henry III (1207-1272)
**Henry III, King of England**
- Reigned 1216-1272
- Son of King John (of Magna Carta fame)
- Father of Edward I "Longshanks"

**Documented lineage from Anne to Henry III:**
- Anne Couvent
- Antoinette de Longueval
- Louise de Joyeuse
- Jean de Joyeuse
- François de Joyeuse
- Marguerite de Barbançon
- François de Barbançon
- Robert de Sarrebruche
- Marie de Chateauvillain
- Jeanne de Grancey
- Yolande de Bar
- Marie de Dampierre
- Marie d'Artois
- Blanche d'Artois
- Beatrice de Dreux
- **Henry III, King of England**

### 3. Holy Roman Empire: Frederick I "Barbarossa" (c.1122-1190)
**Frederick I von Hohenstaufen "Barbarossa", Emperor**
- Ruled 1155-1190
- One of the most powerful Holy Roman Emperors
- Died during the Third Crusade
- "Barbarossa" means "Red Beard"

### 4. Jerusalem: Amalric I (1136-1174)
**Amalric I, King of Jerusalem**
- Ruled 1163-1174
- Crusader King
- Brother of Baldwin III
- Expanded the Crusader Kingdom

### 5. Navarre: Thibault I (1201-1253)
**Thibault I "the Posthumous", King of Navarre**
- Ruled 1234-1253
- Also Count of Champagne
- Patron of troubadours and poets
- Called "posthumous" because his father died before his birth

### 6. Latin Constantinople: Baldwin I (1172-1205)
**Baldwin I, Emperor of Latin Constantinople**
- Ruled 1204-1205
- First Emperor of the Latin Empire after the Fourth Crusade
- Count of Flanders and Hainaut
- Died in captivity fighting the Bulgarians

### 7. Castile and León: Saint Fernando III (1199-1252)
**Saint Fernando III, King of Castile and León**
- Ruled Castile 1217-1252, León 1230-1252
- United the kingdoms permanently
- Reconquered much of Andalusia from the Moors
- Canonized as a saint in 1671

Through these seven royal lineages, Anne Couvent also connects to:

### William the Conqueror (c.1028-1087)
- Through Henry III of England
- William was Henry III's 3rd great-grandfather
- Duke of Normandy, King of England (1066)
- Famous for the Norman Conquest of England

### Charlemagne (742-814)
- Through multiple lines (Louis VIII, Henry III, Barbarossa)
- Charles I "Charlemagne", King of the Franks, Emperor
- Crowned first Holy Roman Emperor (800 AD)
- "Father of Europe"
- United much of Western Europe

**Charlemagne to William the Conqueror:**
- Charlemagne was William the Conqueror's 9th great-grandfather
- Connection through Charles II "the Bald" → Louis I "the Pious" → Charlemagne

### Earlier Connections
Through Charlemagne, lineages extend to:
- Merovingian kings of the Franks
- Roman patrician families
- Byzantine emperors
- Visigothic and Ostrogothic royalty

## Immigration to New France (1636)

### Journey from France

Anne Couvent came to New France from **Picardy** with:
- Her husband **Philippe Amiot** (also called "dit Hameau" or "Sieur de Villeneuve")
- Two young sons: Jean and Mathieu
- Year: **1636** (possibly June 11)

### Early Life in Quebec

**August 26, 1636**: Third son Charles baptized in Quebec - the first child born in New France

**Before September 1639**: Philippe Amiot died
- Survived in New France only 3-4 years
- Left Anne a widow with three young sons

### Second Marriage (1639)

**September 26, 1639**: Anne married **Jacques Maheu**
- Had two children together
- One daughter (Marie Madeleine) died in infancy
- One son (Jean Maheu) survived

Jacques Maheu died before 1666

### Third Marriage (1666)

**1666**: Anne married **Étienne Blanchon**
- No children from this marriage
- She was in her mid-60s by this time

### Death (1675)

**December 25, 1675**: Anne died in Quebec on Christmas Day
- Age approximately 71-74
- Had lived in New France for 39 years
- Outlived two husbands

## Legacy and Descendants

### Massive Descendancy

Through her three sons from her first marriage (Jean, Mathieu, and Charles Amiot) and one son from her second marriage (Jean Maheu), Anne Couvent has **thousands of descendants** throughout Canada and the United States.

### Royal Blood in North America

Many French-Canadians and Americans descend from Anne Couvent and thus carry royal blood from:
- The Kings of France
- The Kings of England
- Holy Roman Emperors
- Crusader Kings
- Spanish royalty
- Byzantine emperors
- And ultimately from Charlemagne himself

### Her Sister's Descendants

Anne's sister **Charlotte Couvent** married Louis Ledran. Their son **Toussaint Ledran** also came to New France.

All descendants of Charlotte Couvent (through Toussaint Ledran) share the same seven royal lineages.

### Gateway Importance

Anne Couvent is significant because:
- **More recent royal connections** than other gateway ancestors
- **Multiple royal lines** (seven documented lineages)
- **Well-documented ancestry** thanks to Gagné and Kokanosky's research
- **Extensive descendancy** affecting thousands of families

## The Noble Ancestry Through Her Mother

### Antoinette de Longueval (c.1582-c.1640)

Anne's mother was born into French nobility:
- Born c.1582 in Soissons, Picardy, France
- Daughter of **Charles de Longueval** and **Louise de Joyeuse**
- Married Guillaume Convent dit Estrée in Soissons
- Died c.1640

### Louise de Joyeuse

Anne's maternal grandmother:
- **Dame of Sivry**
- Married Charles de Longueval, Ecuyer Seigneur de Sivry et de Vuallicourt
- Member of the noble house of Joyeuse
- Connection point to multiple royal lines

The **Joyeuse family** was an important noble house in France:
- Lords and later Dukes of Joyeuse
- Served French kings for generations
- Connected to many other noble families through marriage

### Why This Matters

Anne Couvent herself was not of the highest nobility - her father Guillaume Convent was likely minor gentry or bourgeoisie. But through her **mother's line** (Longueval and Joyeuse), she connected to the high nobility and ultimately to royalty.

This pattern is common: royal blood often descends through female lines, sometimes "hidden" in families of lower status until genealogists uncover the connections.

## Historical Context

### Picardy in the Early 1600s

Anne grew up in **Picardy**, northern France:
- Region included cities like Amiens, Soissons, Beauvais
- Close to Paris and the royal court
- Frequent warfare between France and Spain/Holy Roman Empire
- Many noble families in the region
- Catholic stronghold during Wars of Religion

### Why Immigrate to New France?

In 1636, New France was still very young:
- Quebec City founded in 1608 (only 28 years earlier)
- Harsh conditions, constant threat from Iroquois
- Fur trade economy
- Few French settlers (perhaps 500-600 people)

Why would Anne and Philippe leave France for this frontier?
- **Economic opportunity**: Land and advancement
- **Adventure**: New World possibilities
- **Religious mission**: Converting Indigenous peoples (though Amiots weren't clergy)
- **Escape**: Perhaps troubles in France
- **Noble connections**: Anne's noble ancestry might have helped secure passage

### Life as a Widow in New France

Losing Philippe after only 3-4 years left Anne in a difficult position:
- Widow with three young sons
- Frontier colony with few resources
- Remarriage was almost necessary for survival

That she remarried within months (September 1639) was practical and normal for the time.

## Genealogical Challenges

### Why Was This Hidden So Long?

Anne Couvent's royal connections were unknown until 2007 because:

1. **Female lines are harder to trace**: Noble ancestry through mothers often goes unrecorded
2. **No noble title in New France**: The Amiot family weren't nobility in Quebec
3. **Distant connections**: The royal ancestors were 12-15 generations back
4. **French records**: Required research in French archives, noble genealogies
5. **Skepticism**: Many claimed "royal descent" turned out to be false

### The 2007 Breakthrough

Gagné and Kokanosky's achievement:
- Traced female line from Anne → Antoinette de Longueval → Louise de Joyeuse
- Connected Joyeuse to documented noble families
- Followed lines through multiple European royal houses
- Provided documentary evidence for each generation
- Published in peer-reviewed genealogical journal

This is the gold standard for proving royal descent.

## Other Gateway Ancestors for Comparison

### Catherine de Baillon

- Came to New France as a **Fille du Roi** (King's Daughter) in 1669
- Royal descent through the **Malet de Graville** family
- Connects to earlier medieval royalty
- Many descendants in Quebec

### Jeanne Le Marchand

- Married Jean Le Neuf de la Poterie
- Royal connections through Norman nobility
- Also connects to medieval French kings
- Extensive Quebec descendancy

### Anne Couvent's Advantage

Anne's royal connections are **more recent** than Catherine de Baillon's or Jeanne Le Marchand's:
- Her lines go to 12th-13th century kings
- Their lines go to earlier medieval rulers
- More recent = easier to document
- More recent = feels more "real" to descendants

## The Coats of Arms

Each generation of Anne's royal ancestry bore **heraldic arms** (coats of arms):
- Henry III of England: Three gold lions on red
- Louis VIII of France: Fleur-de-lis on blue
- Frederick Barbarossa: Black eagle on gold
- Etc.

The habitant.org website documents these arms for each generation, showing the visual symbols of Anne's noble ancestry.

Technically, descendants don't inherit the right to use these specific arms (heraldry follows male lines with specific rules), but the connection to armigerous (arms-bearing) ancestors is historically significant.
