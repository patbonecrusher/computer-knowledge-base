# Clément Gosselin

## Personal Information
- **Birth**: June 12, 1747
- **Birthplace**: Sainte-Famille parish, Île d'Orléans, Quebec
- **Death**: March 9, 1816
- **Death Location**: Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York
- **Burial**: East Beekmantown Cemetery (monument erected 2022)
- **Age at Death**: 68 years
- **Parents**: Gabriel Gosselin and Geneviève Crépeaux
- **Occupation**: Carpenter (like his father)
- **Military Rank**: Captain (later promoted to Major in June 1783)
- **Regiment**: Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment, Continental Army

### Marriages
1. **Marie Beuve Dionne** - married January 22, 1770 in Sainte-Anne-de-la-Pocatière (born March 5, 1755)
2. **Marie Charlotte Ouimet** - married January 15, 1787 in Longueuil (born February 10, 1761)
3. **Marie Catherine Monty** - married May 12, 1791 in Saint-Hyacinthe (born July 30, 1769)

### Children
At least 25 children across three marriages (1770-1811)

## Patriot Ancestor Recognition
- **DAR (Daughters of the American Revolution)**: Patriot Ancestor A046437

## Early Life

### Family Background

Clément was born into an established Quebec family. The Gosselin family had deep roots in New France:
- **Ancestor Gabriel Gosselin** emigrated from Normandy, France to Quebec around 1652
- The family had been in Quebec for nearly 100 years by Clément's birth
- His father was a carpenter, a respected trade in colonial Quebec
- He was the youngest of a large family

### Growing Up on Île d'Orléans

Île d'Orléans, just east of Quebec City in the St. Lawrence River, was:
- One of the oldest settled areas of New France
- Agricultural and prosperous
- French-speaking and Catholic
- Close enough to Quebec City to be connected to political events

Clément grew up speaking French, practicing Catholicism, and learning his father's carpentry trade.

### Location: La Pocatière

By the time of the American Revolution, Clément was living in **La Pocatière**, Quebec (on the south shore of the St. Lawrence, about 75 miles northeast of Quebec City).

## The American Revolution Comes to Quebec

### The Context: 1775

In 1775, the American colonies rebelled against British rule. The Continental Congress hoped that Quebec (conquered by Britain from France in 1760) would join the rebellion. Many French-Canadians were unhappy with British rule and sympathetic to the American cause.

### The American Invasion (Fall 1775)

Two American armies invaded Quebec:
1. **Richard Montgomery** came north via Lake Champlain and captured Montreal
2. **Benedict Arnold** led a grueling expedition through the Maine wilderness to Quebec City

The plan was to capture Quebec City and bring Quebec into the rebellion.

### The Battle of Quebec (December 31, 1775)

On the night of December 30-31, 1775, American forces attacked Quebec City in a snowstorm.

**Clément's Participation**:
- At age 28, Clément was one of approximately **200 French-Canadians** who joined the American attack
- This was a significant choice - supporting rebellion against British authority
- The battle was a **disaster for the Americans**:
  - General Richard Montgomery was killed
  - Benedict Arnold was wounded
  - Many captured, including Daniel Morgan
  - The attack failed completely

Despite the defeat, Clément was not deterred. His commitment to the American cause had just begun.

## Recruiting for the Continental Army

### January - May 1776

After the failed attack on Quebec City, Clément took on a dangerous mission: **recruiting French-Canadians for the Continental Army**.

He traveled throughout parishes on the south shore of the St. Lawrence:
- From **Pointe-Lévy** (now Lauzon and Lévis, across from Quebec City)
- To **Sainte-Anne-de-la-Pocatière**
- Visiting French-Canadian communities
- Convincing men to join the American cause

This was extremely risky:
- British authorities considered this treason
- Anyone caught recruiting faced severe punishment
- His family could be punished
- Properties could be seized

But Clément believed in the cause and successfully recruited volunteers.

### Battle of Saint-Pierre (March 25, 1776)

On March 25, 1776, Clément fought in the **Battle of Saint-Pierre**:
- **Pro-American forces**: 150 French-Canadians (including Clément) + 80 Americans
- **Pro-British forces**: 150 French-Canadians recruited by Liénard de Beaujeu
- **Result**: Victory for the pro-American side

This battle showed the division among French-Canadians - some supported the Americans, others the British.

## Captain in the 2nd Canadian Regiment

### March 1776: Commission

In **March 1776**, Clément officially joined **Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment** as **Captain of the 7th Company**.

### About the 2nd Canadian Regiment

The **2nd Canadian Regiment** (also called "Congress's Own" or "Hazen's Regiment") was:
- Formed from French-Canadian volunteers who supported the Revolution
- Commanded by Colonel Moses Hazen (a controversial figure)
- Part of the Continental Army
- Composed primarily of French-speaking Catholics from Quebec
- One of the few regiments representing Canada in the American Revolution

### Recruiting Success

As a captain, Clément continued recruiting. Along with Pierre Ayotte, another recruiter working for Moses Hazen, Clément raised about **150 men** who joined the Americans.

This was an impressive achievement given:
- The failed Quebec invasion
- British control of Quebec
- Risk to anyone who joined
- Catholic Church's general opposition to the rebellion

## George Washington's Spy in Quebec

### Intelligence Operations (1778-1780)

From **summer 1778 until summer 1780**, Clément undertook perhaps his most dangerous work: **returning secretly to Quebec as a spy**.

His mission:
- Gather intelligence on British military strength
- Assess sentiment among French-Canadians
- Spread rumors of an imminent American invasion (with French support)
- Report directly to **George Washington**

### The 1778 Letter to Washington

On **November 23, 1778**, Clément wrote to George Washington detailing:
- His spying activities
- Plans for another invasion attempt on Canada (which never materialized)
- A desperate plea: **"all our possessions were seized and that our families were at the mercy of the world"**

This letter reveals the terrible cost of his commitment:
- His property in Quebec had been confiscated by British authorities
- His family was suffering
- He was asking Washington for help

### The October 1778 Intelligence Report

In October 1778, Clément and his spy network drafted a comprehensive **report on the state of Quebec** for Congress, which was planning another attack on the British in Quebec.

This report made him Washington's **personal Canadian military intelligence informant**.

## The Siege of Yorktown

### October 4, 1781

At the climactic **Siege of Yorktown** - the battle that effectively ended the Revolutionary War - Clément was present with the 2nd Canadian Regiment.

On **October 4, 1781**, he was **severely wounded in the leg** when a cannonball struck nearby, sending wood splinters flying. The injury was serious enough to affect him for the rest of his life.

But he was there when Lord Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781, effectively winning American independence.

### Promotion to Major

At some point during or after his service, Clément was promoted to **Major** - a significant rank reflecting his leadership, recruiting success, and intelligence work.

## The Cost of Loyalty

### Family Persecution

Clément's support for the American Revolution cost his family dearly:

- **Properties seized**: All Gosselin possessions in Quebec were confiscated by British authorities
- **Family persecuted**: His relatives left in Quebec faced harassment and hardship
- **Unable to return**: Clément could not safely return to British-controlled Quebec during or immediately after the war
- **Economic hardship**: His family was left "at the mercy of the world"

This was the price of supporting revolution against British authority.

### After the War

After American independence was won in 1783, Clément's situation was complicated:
- The Americans had won, but Canada remained British
- His properties in Quebec were still confiscated
- His family had suffered for his choices
- He was recognized as a patriot by the Americans, but considered a traitor by the British

## Later Life and Death

### Post-War Life (1783-1816)

**Promotion and Discharge (June 1783):**
- Promoted to **Major** in June 1783 after Treaty of Versailles
- Honorably discharged from Continental Army
- **Important**: Promotion not properly recorded in regimental records, causing pension issues

**New York Land Grant (1784):**
- Received **1,000 acres** on shores of Lake Champlain from New York State
- Veterans' reward for Canadian soldiers of Continental Army
- Sold the land soon after, preferring to return to carpentry

**Champlain, New York (1783-1791):**
- Lived at Champlain, NY (on Quebec border)
- Worked as carpenter
- Married second wife Marie Charlotte Ouimet (1787)
- Had three children with her
- Lived as close to Quebec as safely possible

**Return to Quebec (1791-1815):**
- **1791-1800**: Moved to Saint-Hyacinthe region, Lower Canada
  - Married third wife Marie Catherine Monty (May 1791)
  - Had many children
  - Resumed carpentry work
- **~1800**: Briefly at Sainte-Marguerite-de-Blairfindie (L'Acadie)
- **1803-1815**: Settled at Saint-Luc for 12 years
  - Continued having children (last born 1811)
  - Lived quietly as an old revolutionary

**Final Move (1815):**
- Moved to Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York at age 67
- Reasons unknown (possibly for pension, to be with veterans, or because he was dying)

**Death (March 9, 1816):**
- Died in Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York at age 68
- Buried in East Beekmantown Cemetery
- Grave unmarked for over 200 years (monument erected 2022)

### Pension Injustice and Congressional Redress

**The Problem:**
- Clément's promotion to Major was not properly recorded
- His half-pay pension calculated at Captain rank instead of Major
- Significant financial loss for him and his widow

**Resolution (1860):**
- His children and heirs petitioned Congress after his death
- Congressional Committee on Revolutionary Claims examined evidence
- Testimony from Jean Monty (regimental fifer who served with Clément)
- Committee concluded Clément had been wronged
- Ordered difference in pay granted to heirs
- This was **44 years after his death**, on eve of Civil War

### Historical Significance

Clément represents:
- **French-Canadian support for American Revolution**: Often overlooked in history
- **Complexity of Quebec during Revolution**: Divided loyalties, difficult choices
- **Personal sacrifice for ideals**: Lost everything for his beliefs
- **Intelligence operations**: Crucial spying work for Washington
- **International dimension**: The Revolution as more than just 13 colonies

### Often Forgotten

Despite his significant contributions:
- The Canadian/Quebec role in the American Revolution is often minimized
- The 2nd Canadian Regiment is less famous than other Continental units
- French-Canadian patriots like Clément are underappreciated in both US and Canadian history

### Recent Recognition (2024)

The Quebec government's 2024 designation of Clément as a historic figure suggests renewed interest in:
- Quebec's complex relationship with the American Revolution
- French-Canadian patriots who supported independence
- The costs paid by those who chose the losing side in Quebec

## Historical Context

### Quebec and the American Revolution

When the American Revolution began in 1775, Quebec had been under British rule for only 15 years (since the British conquest in 1760). Many French-Canadians:
- Resented British rule
- Hoped for restoration of French control
- Were sympathetic to American ideals of liberty
- But were also wary of Protestant Americans
- Faced pressure from the Catholic Church (which generally opposed rebellion)

The American invasion of 1775-1776 found some support (like Clément) but ultimately failed due to:
- Harsh winter conditions
- Strong British defenses
- Insufficient French-Canadian support
- Catholic Church opposition
- Failed military tactics

### The 2nd Canadian Regiment

Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment was unique:
- French-speaking Catholics in a primarily English Protestant army
- Recruited from Quebec but couldn't return there
- Served throughout the war despite little hope of liberating their homeland
- Many married American women and settled in the US after the war

### Spying in the Revolutionary War

Intelligence operations were crucial to the Revolution:
- Washington relied heavily on spy networks
- Information about British movements and strength was vital
- Agents like Clément risked everything for the cause
- The Culper Spy Ring and others are famous; Clément's Quebec network less so
