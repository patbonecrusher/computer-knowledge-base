# Louis Badaillac dit Laplante - 1665

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/louis-badaillac|Louis Badaillac Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

Two founding moments. Two generations. One family.

On June 19, 1665, after two months at sea, the Dutch ship *Le Vieux Simeon* glided up the St. Lawrence River toward Quebec City. Among the soldiers crowded on deck, straining for their first glimpse of New France, stood a young man of about twenty-one years from Périgord. Louis Badaillac had left behind a France torn by civil war to become part of one of the most significant military deployments in New France's history: the Carignan-Salières Regiment.

Thirty-six years later, in 1701, another young Louis Badaillac—the first Louis's son—would paddle away from Montreal in one of twenty-five canoes, part of Cadillac's convoy to establish Detroit.

Father and son. Quebec and Detroit. Both present at the creation.

This is their story.

---

## Life in Périgord

### The Place He Called Home

Louis Badaillac was born around 1644 in the diocese of Périgueux, in the heart of Périgord (today's Dordogne department). This region in southwestern France was known for its rolling hills, rich farmland, and strategic location along ancient trade routes. The city of Périgueux itself was an old Gallo-Roman settlement, with layers of history visible in its medieval streets and Roman ruins.

But the Périgord of Louis's childhood was not a peaceful place.

### A France Divided: The Fronde

When Louis was just four years old, in 1648, France erupted in civil war. The conflict, known as the Fronde, pitted nobles and regional powers against the young King Louis XIV and his regent mother, Anne of Austria. It was a time of chaos, shifting allegiances, and violence that touched every corner of France—including Périgord.

Périgueux, like many French cities, was caught up in these disturbances. The fighting lasted from 1648 to 1653, throughout Louis Badaillac's formative years. For a boy growing up during this time, the experience would have been one of instability and uncertainty. Armies marched through the countryside. Loyalties were tested. The future was unclear.

It wasn't until 1654—when Louis was ten years old—that King Louis XIV granted amnesty to Périgueux for its role in the Fronde. Finally, peace came to the region. After generations of turmoil (the Hundred Years' War, the Wars of Religion, and now the Fronde), Périgueux could begin to rebuild.

But for young Louis, the damage was done. He had grown up in a world of conflict, instability, and limited prospects.

### A Young Man's Options

We don't know Louis Badaillac's parents' names, nor what his father did for a living. No marriage contract or parish record has survived to tell us about his family background. This suggests they were likely common people—farmers, tradesmen, or laborers—not nobility or wealthy merchants whose records would have been carefully preserved.

For a young man in his position in 1664, options were limited. France was still recovering from years of civil war. Land was scarce. Opportunities were few. But there was one avenue that promised adventure, steady pay, and the chance at a new life: the army.

And in 1664, King Louis XIV was recruiting heavily for a special mission to New France.

## The Decision to Leave

### The Call to New France

In the early 1660s, New France was in crisis. The Iroquois Confederacy, particularly the Mohawks, had been launching devastating raids on French settlements for decades. Colonists lived in constant fear. Trade was disrupted. The colony's survival was in question.

King Louis XIV, having consolidated his power after the Fronde, decided to act decisively. He would send not just a few dozen soldiers, but an entire regiment—over 1,200 men—to crush the Iroquois threat once and for all.

The Carignan-Salières Regiment was chosen for this mission. Originally two separate regiments that had merged in 1659, these soldiers had fought in Hungary against the Ottoman Turks. Now they would cross the Atlantic to fight in the forests of North America.

Recruitment began in 1664. Officers fanned out across France, seeking soldiers willing to make the dangerous journey. For young men like Louis Badaillac, the offer was compelling:

- Steady pay as a soldier
- Land grants after service
- A chance to start fresh in a new world
- Escape from a France still recovering from civil war

Louis, about twenty years old and from a region that had been devastated by the Fronde, signed up.

### What He Left Behind

Louis Badaillac's decision meant leaving everything he knew. The fields and hills of Périgord. Whatever family he had—parents, siblings, cousins. The dialect of southwestern France. The food, the culture, the familiar rhythms of life.

In that era, leaving for New France was likely permanent. The voyage was expensive and dangerous. Few soldiers ever returned to France. When Louis boarded the ship at La Rochelle in April 1665, he was, in effect, saying goodbye forever to his homeland.

But perhaps, given the instability he'd grown up with, the prospect of building something new was worth the sacrifice.

### What He Hoped to Find

New France promised opportunity. For soldiers who completed their service and chose to stay, the colony offered:

- Land grants (often 40-60 acres)
- Tools and supplies to start farming
- Potential marriage to one of the "Filles du Roi" (King's Daughters) being sent from France
- A chance to be among the founders of a new society

For a common-born young man from war-torn Périgord, this was an extraordinary opportunity.

## The Voyage

### The Ship: Le Vieux Simeon

In April 1665, Louis Badaillac made his way to La Rochelle, France's primary Atlantic port. There he boarded *Le Vieux Simeon* (The Old Simeon), a chartered Dutch ship commanded by Captain Pierre-André de Froment.

**Ship Details:**
- **Type**: Chartered Dutch vessel
- **Departure**: La Rochelle, France - April 19, 1665
- **Arrival**: Quebec City - June 19, 1665
- **Duration**: Exactly 2 months
- **Passengers**: Soldiers of the Carignan-Salières Regiment, Froment Company
- **Companion Ships**: *La Justice* and *La Paix* also carried regiment soldiers

### Two Months at Sea

The Atlantic crossing in the 17th century was grueling. Soldiers were packed into cramped quarters below deck. The diet consisted mainly of salted meat, ship's biscuit (often infested with weevils), and whatever water remained drinkable. Storms were terrifying. Disease spread quickly in the close quarters. Men died en route.

For two months, Louis and his fellow soldiers endured these conditions. They drilled when weather permitted, maintaining discipline and preparing for the campaign ahead. They told stories, played dice, formed the bonds that would serve them in battle.

As they sailed west into the unknown, Louis must have wondered what awaited him. None of these soldiers had seen the forests of North America. None had faced the Iroquois in battle. None knew whether they would survive the coming conflict.

But on June 19, 1665, land appeared on the horizon: the rocky shores of the St. Lawrence.

### Arrival in the New World

Quebec City in 1665 was hardly a city—more a fortified settlement of perhaps 550 people, clustered on the heights above the St. Lawrence River. As *Le Vieux Simeon* approached, Louis would have seen:

- The wooden palisades protecting the upper town
- The spire of Notre-Dame-de-Québec church
- The handful of stone buildings among mostly wooden structures
- The vast, endless forests stretching in every direction

This was New France: wild, dangerous, and full of possibility.

## Military Service: The Carignan-Salières Campaign

### The Regiment's Mission

Louis Badaillac was part of an overwhelming show of force. During summer and fall 1665, about 1,200-1,300 soldiers and 80 officers of the Carignan-Salières Regiment arrived in Quebec. It was the largest military deployment New France had ever seen.

Their mission was threefold:
1. Intimidate the Iroquois Confederacy
2. Build forts to secure French territory
3. If necessary, invade Iroquois lands and crush their military power

The regiment was under the overall command of:
- **Lieutenant Général Alexandre de Prouville de Tracy** - the supreme military authority in New France
- **Governor General Daniel de Rémy de Courcelle**
- **Colonel Henri de Chastelard de Salières**

Louis Badaillac served in the **Froment Company**, named for his ship's captain, Pierre-André de Froment.

### First Winter: Trois-Rivières

Upon arrival, Louis and his company were first sent to Trois-Rivières (Three Rivers), about halfway between Quebec and Montreal. Here they spent their first winter, adapting to a climate unlike anything they'd experienced in southwestern France.

Winter in New France was brutal. Temperatures plummeted far below freezing. Snow piled in drifts. The St. Lawrence froze solid. For soldiers from France, this was an ordeal. Many fell ill. Some died. All suffered.

But they survived, and in the summer of 1666, they received their orders.

### Building Fort Saint-Louis (Chambly)

In July 1666, Louis Badaillac and his fellow soldiers were charged with a critical task: constructing Fort Saint-Louis along the Richelieu River (the waterway the Iroquois used to raid French settlements). This fort would later be known as Fort Chambly.

This was backbreaking work. The soldiers felled trees, shaped logs, dug foundations, and raised palisades. They worked in the summer heat, plagued by mosquitoes and black flies. They remained constantly alert for Iroquois attacks.

But Fort Saint-Louis rose on the Richelieu, a powerful statement of French military presence and a barrier against Iroquois raids.

### The Iroquois Campaigns

The Carignan-Salières Regiment took part in three expeditions against the Iroquois in 1666. These campaigns involved marching through dense forests, along waterways, into Iroquois territory. The goal was not so much to fight pitched battles as to demonstrate overwhelming force—to burn Iroquois villages and food stores, to show that the French could strike deep into their lands.

The strategy worked. The Iroquois, faced with this show of strength, agreed to negotiate.

On **July 10, 1667**, peace was declared between the French and the Five Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy. It was a peace that would last eighteen years—nearly a generation.

### Confirmation at Chambly

On May 20, 1669, Louis Badaillac received the sacrament of Confirmation at Fort Saint-Louis de Chambly. The record notes him as "Louis Badaillac, de l'évêché de Périgueux" (from the diocese of Périgueux). This religious ceremony may seem like a small detail, but it tells us Louis was still there, still serving, four years after his arrival.

And it suggests he was beginning to put down roots.

## Building a New Life

### The Decision to Stay

In 1668, the Carignan-Salières Regiment received orders to return to France. But King Louis XIV and his ministers had a different plan for those willing to remain: stay in New France, receive land grants, marry, and help populate the colony.

The incentives were generous. About 400-450 officers and soldiers chose to stay—including Louis Badaillac.

For Louis, now in his mid-twenties, this must have been a carefully considered choice. Return to France, to what? The same limited prospects he'd left behind? Or stay in this harsh but promising land, claim land of his own, and build something new?

He chose to stay.

### Marriage to Catherine Lalore

Around 1672, in the village of Sorel (a settlement at the mouth of the Richelieu River, near where he'd served), Louis Badaillac married Catherine Lalore.

We know frustratingly little about Catherine. Who were her parents? Was she born in France or New France? How did Louis meet her?

No marriage contract survives. No marriage record has been found. This was not uncommon in early Quebec, where record-keeping was inconsistent and fires, floods, and simple neglect have destroyed many early documents.

But the marriage happened, and from it came children who would spread the Badaillac name (often recorded as "dit Laplante") throughout Quebec.

### The "dit" Name: Laplante

In Quebec, Louis became known as Louis Badaillac **dit Laplante**.

The "dit" (meaning "called" or "known as") was a Quebec tradition, especially common among soldiers. These nicknames helped distinguish between soldiers with the same surname, or simply became part of a family's identity. "Laplante" suggests a connection to farming or planting—perhaps reflecting Louis's new life as a settler working the land.

Over time, many descendants would use "Laplante" as their primary surname, though the "Badaillac" origin remained in some records.

### Settling in Sorel

Louis and Catherine established themselves in or around Sorel. This location made sense: Louis had served in the Richelieu River valley, building forts and establishing a military presence. He knew the area. He likely received his land grant there.

Sorel, founded in 1642, was one of the strategic settlements protecting the approach to Montreal. It was a small community of fellow soldiers-turned-settlers, habitants working their land, and families building a new society.

Life was hard. Clearing land. Building a house. Planting crops. Surviving the brutal winters. Raising children. But it was *his* land, *his* family, *his* life—something impossible in the France he'd left behind.

### Their Children

Louis and Catherine had eight children—a large family even by the standards of New France, where big families were the norm. Seven survived to adulthood and married, establishing the Badaillac/Laplante name throughout Quebec and beyond.

**Marie Catherine** (baptized 1673) - Their firstborn, she married Pierre Lamy in 1689 when she was about sixteen.

**Marie** (born 1675), also called Marie Madeleine - She married Bernard Delomé in 1694 at St-Laurent on Île d'Orléans.

**Anne Élisabeth** (born 1677) - Tragically, she died young at age twelve in 1690. Her death would have been a terrible grief for Louis and Catherine.

**Louis** (born 1680) - Their first surviving son would go on to be part of one of the most important expeditions in North American history. At age 21, young Louis joined Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac's convoy to found Detroit in 1701. When Cadillac's convoy of 25 canoes and 100 people departed Montreal on June 5, 1701, Louis Badaillac (fils) was among them. On July 24, 1701, they established Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit—what we now know as Detroit, Michigan. He served as a voyageur there from 1701 to 1703.

**Gilles** (born 1682) - He became known as Gilles Badaillac dit Laplante and married three times: first to Françoise Giguière in 1705, then to Marguerite Modoue in 1722, and finally to Marie Claude Minville in 1725. Through his multiple marriages and children, he helped spread the Laplante name widely.

**Thérèse** (baptized 1685) - She married Pierre Guignard in January 1705, just months after her father's death.

**Claire** (born around 1686) - She married François Lancteau in 1707 when she was twenty-one.

**Catherine** (baptized 1690) - The youngest, baptized the same year her sister Anne Élisabeth died. She married young—only sixteen—to Bonaventure Compain dit L'Espérance in 1706 in Montreal.

Eight children born between 1673 and 1690, in the settlement of Sorel, in a new land their father had helped secure from the Iroquois threat. Through these children and their children's children, the Badaillac/Laplante line spread through Quebec and eventually beyond—to Detroit through Louis, throughout the St. Lawrence valley, to New England during later migrations, across North America.

### Death

Both Louis Badaillac and Catherine Lalore died before 1705, the year their daughter Thérèse married. We don't know how or when they died, or where they're buried. Their burial records, like their marriage record, have been lost to time.

But they lived long enough to accomplish something remarkable. Between 1673 and 1690, they raised eight children in the wilderness of New France. They saw their eldest daughters marry and start families of their own. They endured the heartbreak of losing young Anne Élisabeth in 1690. They watched their sons grow strong.

Louis died knowing he had helped secure New France from the Iroquois and had established a family line that would endure. He may even have known, before his death, that his son Louis was planning to join Cadillac's expedition west. The soldier from Périgord who arrived in 1665 became the father of a Detroit founder—a legacy that spanned two of New France's most important moments.

## Legacy

### From Soldier to Founder

Louis Badaillac was one of approximately 400-450 Carignan-Salières soldiers who stayed in New France. This group formed a crucial population boost for the struggling colony. They brought:

- Military experience and discipline
- Labor to clear land and build settlements
- Men of marrying age (many wed Filles du Roi)
- Increased security along the frontier

Historians estimate that today, a significant proportion of French-Canadians—perhaps 20-30%—descend from Carignan-Salières soldiers. Louis Badaillac was one of the founders, one of the men who turned New France from a precarious outpost into a viable society.

### The Detroit Connection: A Father and Son Legacy

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of Louis Badaillac's legacy is that he and his son both participated in two of the most important founding moments in North American history.

**Louis Badaillac (father)** - Part of the Carignan-Salières Regiment (1665) that secured New France from the Iroquois threat and enabled the colony to flourish.

**Louis Badaillac (son)** - Part of the Cadillac Convoy (1701) that founded Detroit, establishing French presence in the Great Lakes region and creating what would become one of North America's great cities.

From soldier in Quebec to voyageur in Detroit, the Badaillac family was literally on the frontier of New France's expansion. When young Louis paddled away from Montreal in 1701, his father—then in his late fifties—must have felt both pride and perhaps nostalgia, remembering his own departure from La Rochelle thirty-six years earlier on *Le Vieux Simeon*.

Both journeys into the unknown. Both acts of courage. Both contributions to building a new world.

### The Badaillac/Laplante Line

From Louis and Catherine descended generations of Laplanters (and sometimes Badaillacs, or the combined Badaillac dit Laplante). His descendants:

- Spread throughout Quebec
- Pioneered the settlement of Detroit (through his son)
- Became farmers, tradesmen, soldiers, clergy
- Some moved to New England during economic migrations
- Some moved west as Canada expanded
- Carried forward both the Badaillac and Laplante names

### Why His Story Matters

Louis Badaillac's story is, in many ways, the quintessential immigrant story. He left a place of limited opportunity and instability. He endured a dangerous journey. He faced hardship and uncertainty in a new land. But through courage, hard work, and a willingness to build rather than retreat, he created a legacy that lasted centuries.

His story is also deeply Canadian—and American. The Carignan-Salières Regiment was a pivotal moment in Canadian history—the military force that secured peace with the Iroquois and enabled the colony to grow. The soldiers who stayed, like Louis, literally built the foundation of Quebec society. And through his son, Louis's legacy extended to the founding of Detroit, one of the most important cities in American history.

How many families can claim both Quebec and Detroit in their founding story? The Badaillac family was there for both.

And his story is personal. He is your ancestor. His blood runs in your veins. His decision to stay in New France, to marry Catherine, to raise children in Sorel—including a son brave enough to venture even further west—that decision created the line that eventually led to you.

When you walk through old Quebec, when you visit Detroit, when you read about the Richelieu River valley, when you encounter the name "Laplante" in your family tree, remember Louis Badaillac: soldier, settler, founder, father, ancestor.

## Historical Context: The Carignan-Salières Regiment in New France

### The Mission
- **1665**: About 1,200-1,300 soldiers and 80 officers arrived
- **Objective**: Eliminate the Iroquois threat to New France
- **Strategy**: Build forts, show overwhelming force, secure peace

### The Forts
The regiment constructed a chain of fortifications along the Richelieu River:
- Fort Richelieu (1665)
- Fort Saint-Louis/Chambly (1665)
- Fort Sainte-Thérèse (1665)
- Fort Saint-Jean (1666)

These forts secured the water route and protected settlements.

### The Campaigns
Three military expeditions into Iroquois territory in 1666 demonstrated French power without major battles. The strategy of overwhelming force worked.

### The Peace
July 10, 1667: Peace treaty with the Five Nations lasted 18 years—critical breathing room for the colony to grow.

### The Settlers
- ~400-450 soldiers stayed
- Many married Filles du Roi (King's Daughters sent from France)
- Received land grants (typically 40-60 acres)
- Became the foundation of Quebec's population
- Today: 20-30% of French-Canadians descend from these soldiers

### The Impact
The Carignan-Salières Regiment fundamentally changed New France. Before 1665: a struggling, threatened outpost. After 1668: a secure, growing society with a critical mass of settlers, cleared land, and military security.

## Questions That Remain

Despite our research, mysteries remain about Louis Badaillac:

- **Who were his parents?** No record survives naming them.
- **What did his father do?** Was Louis from a farming family? Tradesmen?
- **Did he have siblings?** Did any of them come to New France?
- **Exactly when did he marry Catherine?** Around 1672, but no precise record.
- **Who were Catherine Lalore's parents?** Her origins are as mysterious as Louis's.
- **How many children did they have?** We know of daughter Thérèse, but likely there were others.
- **When and how did Louis die?** Before 1705, but where and how is unknown.
- **Where are they buried?** No burial records or grave markers have been found.

These gaps are frustrating but typical of 17th-century Quebec genealogy. Records were inconsistent. Fires destroyed parish registers. Not every birth, marriage, and death was recorded.

But what we do know—his origins in Périgueux, his voyage on *Le Vieux Simeon*, his service with the Carignan-Salières, his marriage to Catherine, his decision to stay—paints a vivid picture of a young man who helped build a new world.
