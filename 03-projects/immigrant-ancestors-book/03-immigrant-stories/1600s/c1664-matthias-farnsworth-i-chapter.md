# Matthias Farnsworth I - c. 1664

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/matthias-farnsworth-i|Matthias Farnsworth I Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

Around 1664, a weaver named Matthias Farnsworth arrived in Groton, Massachusetts—a tiny frontier settlement carved from the wilderness, perpetually vulnerable to attack, thirty-five miles from the relative safety of Boston.

He came from Lynn, another Massachusetts town. Before that, perhaps from Eccles, Lancashire, England—though we cannot be certain. Ship records don't survive. Documentary evidence linking him to England remains elusive.

But we know this: Matthias Farnsworth became an original proprietor of Groton, owned a twenty-acre right, practiced his trade as a weaver, raised ten children, and lived to the remarkable age of seventy-seven.

He could not have imagined that his great-grandson would be captured by Abenaki warriors, converted to Catholicism, and become the founding ancestor of an entirely different family name—Phaneuf—in French Canada.

This is the story of the English weaver whose bloodline would bridge two worlds.

---

## The Mystery of Origins

### Lancashire, England?

A Matthias Farnsworth was baptized on August 20, 1615, in Eccles, Lancashire, England, son of Richard Farnsworth and Elizabeth Marsh.

The dates align. The location makes sense—Lancashire sent many emigrants to New England. And the name "Matthias" was unusual enough that it seems unlikely two different Matthias Farnsworths would appear in the same period.

But no documentary evidence definitively links Matthias Farnsworth of Groton, Massachusetts, to Richard and Elizabeth Farnsworth of Eccles, England.

No ship record lists his passage. No land sale in Lancashire records his departure. No letter or diary confirms the connection.

We are left with probability, not certainty. Most likely, the weaver who arrived in Groton was indeed the baptized child from Eccles. But it remains unproven.

### The Great Migration

Whether from Lancashire specifically or elsewhere in England, Matthias Farnsworth was part of the **Great Migration**—the movement of approximately 20,000 Puritans from England to New England between 1620 and 1640.

These emigrants fled:
- Religious persecution of Puritans by the Church of England
- Economic hardship in a changing England
- Political instability during the English Civil War era
- Lack of land and opportunity

They sought:
- Freedom to practice their strict Protestant faith
- Land ownership (impossible for most in England)
- Economic opportunity
- A chance to build a "city upon a hill"—a godly society

Matthias, like thousands of others, crossed the Atlantic—a dangerous voyage of 6-10 weeks in cramped, disease-ridden conditions. Many died on the passage. He survived.

### Arrival in New England

We don't know exactly when Matthias arrived in Massachusetts or on which ship. His first three children were likely born in England before immigration, suggesting he came as a married man with a young family, probably in the 1640s or early 1650s.

By **1657**, he was established in **Lynn, Massachusetts**, one of the early towns of Massachusetts Bay Colony, founded in 1629.

## Lynn and the Move to Groton

### Lynn, Massachusetts (by 1657)

Lynn was a coastal town northeast of Boston. By the 1650s, it was an established community with:
- Church and minister
- Town government
- Farming and fishing economy
- Relative safety (close to Boston, not on the frontier)

Matthias worked as a **weaver** in Lynn—an essential trade. Weavers:
- Produced cloth for clothing, bedding, household goods
- Needed significant skill and equipment (a loom was a major investment)
- Were valued in the community
- Could earn steady income

For about seven years (possibly longer), Matthias and his family lived in Lynn. His wife Mary bore children. He worked his loom. They attended church. They built a life.

But by 1664, Matthias had moved to the frontier.

### Why Groton?

Groton was founded in 1655, carved from wilderness about 35 miles northwest of Boston. Why would an established weaver in Lynn leave relative safety for the dangerous frontier?

Possible reasons:
- **Land**: Original proprietors received substantial land grants. A twenty-acre right in Groton was more land than he could get in Lynn.
- **Opportunity**: New towns needed skilled tradesmen. A weaver would be valued.
- **Family**: Perhaps relatives or friends had already settled there.
- **Faith**: Groton offered a chance to build a new church community.

Whatever his reasons, Matthias Farnsworth became one of Groton's founding settlers.

---

## Original Proprietor of Groton

### What It Meant

Being an **original proprietor** was significant. It meant Matthias:
- Was among the first settlers of Groton
- Received a land grant from the colonial government
- Had voting rights in town affairs
- Helped shape the town's development
- Would be remembered as a founder

His **twenty-acre right** was substantial property, enabling him to:
- Farm for subsistence and income
- Pass land to his sons
- Establish the family in the community
- Build equity and security

### A Weaver on the Frontier

Matthias continued his trade in Groton. As a weaver, he:
- Provided essential cloth for the community
- Worked his loom in addition to farming his land
- Taught his sons the trade (possibly)
- Contributed specialized skills the frontier desperately needed

Frontier towns couldn't survive on farming alone—they needed blacksmiths, carpenters, coopers, weavers, and other artisans to create the goods necessary for survival.

## Life in Groton: Survival and Tragedy

### The Frontier Reality

Groton in the 1660s-1670s was precarious:
- **Small population**: Perhaps a few dozen families
- **Dense wilderness**: Forests pressing in on all sides
- **Isolation**: Days of travel from Boston
- **Constant danger**: From wildlife, weather, and human threats

Every day required:
- Hard physical labor (farming, building, repairing)
- Vigilance (watching for threats)
- Mutual support (neighbors depending on each other)
- Faith (Puritans found meaning in hardship as God's will)

### King Philip's War (1675-1678)

When Matthias was about sixty years old, disaster struck.

**King Philip's War** (named for Metacomet, a Wampanoag leader called "King Philip" by the English) was the deadliest war in American history proportional to population. Approximately:
- 600 English colonists killed
- 3,000 Native Americans killed
- 12 of 90 Puritan towns destroyed
- More towns heavily damaged

For three years, New England burned.

### Groton's Destruction (March 1676)

On **March 13, 1676**, Groton was attacked and burned. Native American forces overwhelmed the town's defenses. Buildings went up in flames. Residents fled to safer towns or were killed.

The town Matthias had helped build for twelve years was destroyed in a single day.

Most residents abandoned Groton. The town was essentially uninhabited for several years.

But eventually, survivors began to return. They rebuilt. Matthias, now in his sixties, was part of that rebuilding. The weaver from England (or his English-born sons) helped resurrect Groton from ashes.

## Family and Legacy

### Wife and Children

**Wife**: Mary Farr

We know frustratingly little about Mary:
- No birth date or place
- No information about her parents
- No death date
- She exists in records only as "Mary, wife of Matthias"

But she bore and raised **ten children** on the frontier—an extraordinary feat requiring:
- Strength and endurance through multiple pregnancies
- Skill managing a household in primitive conditions
- Courage facing constant danger
- Faith sustaining her through unimaginable hardships

### The Children

Ten children—a large family even by colonial standards—including:

**Matthias Farnsworth Jr.** (born c. 1649)
- The second generation
- Married Sarah Nutting in 1681
- Assigned to the Farnsworth garrison in 1692
- Died in 1693 during the Indian wars
- Father of Matthias III, who would become Claude-Mathias Phaneuf

[Other children's names have been lost to time or require further research]

Through these ten children, the Farnsworth name spread throughout New England. Dozens of grandchildren, hundreds of great-grandchildren, eventually thousands of descendants.

### Death at Age 77

Matthias Farnsworth died on **January 21, 1689**, in Groton, Massachusetts, at approximately **seventy-seven years old**.

This was a remarkably long life for the era. Most colonists died much younger. That he survived:
- The Atlantic crossing
- Frontier hardships
- King Philip's War
- Disease, accidents, and all the dangers of 17th-century life

...to die in his bed at 77 is testament to his constitution and fortune.

He died just four years before his son Matthias Jr. would be killed in the ongoing Indian wars. He didn't live to see his grandson Matthias III captured in 1704.

But he died having done what he set out to do: establish a family in New England.

## Legacy: From England to Quebec

### The English Line

Matthias Farnsworth I established a prominent New England family. His descendants:
- Spread throughout Massachusetts and beyond
- Became farmers, artisans, merchants, ministers
- Married into other founding families
- Helped build New England society

The Farnsworth name became associated with Groton and the surrounding area for generations.

### The Unexpected Branch

But one branch of his family took an entirely unexpected turn.

Through his son Matthias Jr. and grandson Matthias III (Claude-Mathias Phaneuf), the English weaver became:
- The great-grandfather of all Phaneufs in North America
- A link between English Puritan and French Catholic cultures
- An ancestor claimed by both the Farnsworth and Phaneuf families

When 14-year-old Matthias III was captured in 1704 and transformed into Claude-Mathias Phaneuf, the English weaver's bloodline entered Quebec, converting from Protestant to Catholic, from English to French.

### What Would He Have Thought?

If Matthias Farnsworth could have known that his great-grandson would:
- Be captured by Abenaki warriors (enemies he may have fought during King Philip's War)
- Convert to Catholicism (the Puritans' feared and hated papist faith)
- Take a French name and French identity
- Marry a French woman
- Raise French-speaking, Catholic children

...what would he have thought?

Would he have been horrified? Would he have seen it as tragic loss? Would he have understood the survival and adaptation his great-grandson demonstrated?

We'll never know. But the weaver from Lancashire (or wherever he came from) left a legacy more complex than he could have imagined.

## Historical Context: Groton and the Frontier

### Original Proprietors

The proprietor system was how Massachusetts Bay Colony encouraged settlement:
- Groups of men petitioned for grants of wilderness land
- They received the grant and became "proprietors"
- They had rights to allocate land, create town government, and establish churches
- They took on enormous risk in exchange for opportunity

Matthias was one of these risk-takers.

### Puritan Society

Life in Puritan Massachusetts revolved around:
- **The Church**: Attendance mandatory, sermons lasting hours
- **The Bible**: Center of education and life
- **Moral Discipline**: Community oversight of behavior
- **Covenant Theology**: Belief in a special relationship with God
- **Suspicion of Others**: Catholics, Quakers, and religious dissenters were feared

This was the world Matthias knew and the world his great-grandson would leave behind.

### The Weaver's Trade

Weaving in colonial New England was:
- Essential (colonists needed cloth)
- Skilled (required years of training)
- Equipment-intensive (looms were expensive and complex)
- Usually passed from father to son
- Respected work (artisans ranked above laborers but below merchants/ministers)

Matthias's trade gave him status and security.

## Why His Story Matters

Matthias Farnsworth I matters because he represents:

**Courage**: Leaving England for an unknown wilderness

**Resilience**: Surviving the frontier, King Philip's War, and seventy-seven years of hardship

**Foundation**: Helping establish Groton and the Farnsworth family in New England

**Complexity**: How family lines cross boundaries we think of as fixed—English becomes French, Protestant becomes Catholic, New England becomes Quebec

**Connection**: The link that ties your English Puritan heritage to your French-Canadian Catholic heritage

When you think of your Phaneuf ancestors, remember it began with an English weaver in a frontier town, raising ten children in the wilderness.
