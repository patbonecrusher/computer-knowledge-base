# Matthias Farnsworth II - 1681 (Marriage/Settlement)

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/matthias-farnsworth-ii|Matthias Farnsworth II Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

March 1, 1681. Groton, Massachusetts. Thirty-two-year-old Matthias Farnsworth Jr. married seventeen-year-old Sarah Nutting, daughter of another founding family.

It was a moment of hope after years of horror. King Philip's War had ended just three years earlier. Groton, burned to the ground in 1676, was slowly being rebuilt. Life could perhaps return to something resembling normalcy.

They would have twelve years together. Six children. Then, in 1693, Matthias would die in the ongoing Indian wars, leaving Sarah a widow with young children—including a three-year-old son named Matthias III.

That little boy, who would barely remember his father, would be captured by Abenaki warriors eleven years later. He would become Claude-Mathias Phaneuf, the founding ancestor of an entirely new family in French Canada.

But in March 1681, as Matthias and Sarah stood before the minister, none of this could be known. There was only hope for a future together.

---

## A Child of Two Worlds

### Born in Transition

Matthias Farnsworth Jr. was born around 1649—likely in England, as one of his father's first three children born before the family immigrated to New England.

If born in England:
- His earliest years were English
- He crossed the Atlantic as an infant or toddler
- He would have no memories of the old world
- He was fully formed by New England

His entire conscious life was American, Puritan, frontier.

### Growing Up in Lynn and Groton

Young Matthias grew up in:
- **Lynn, Massachusetts** (until about age 15)
- **Groton, Massachusetts** (from about 1664 onward)

As the son of an original proprietor and weaver, he grew up with:
- **Puritan religious education**: Church every Sunday, often multiple times. Bible study. Catechism. Fear of God and awareness of sin.
- **Frontier skills**: Farming, hunting, building. How to clear land, plant crops, repair tools. Survival in the wilderness.
- **Militia training**: From adolescence onward, all able males trained in weapons and tactics. The frontier required every man to be a soldier.

This was his normal—hard work, religious discipline, and constant awareness of danger.

---

## King Philip's War: Coming of Age in Blood

### The War Begins (1675)

When Matthias Jr. was about twenty-six years old, **King Philip's War** erupted across New England.

Metacomet (called "King Philip" by the English), sachem of the Wampanoag, led a coalition of Indigenous nations against English expansion. For three years (1675-1678), New England burned.

The violence was shocking:
- Entire towns destroyed
- Hundreds of colonists killed
- Thousands of Native Americans killed
- Atrocities on all sides
- A war of extermination

Matthias Jr., in his mid-to-late twenties, would have fought. There was no choice—every able man was needed for defense and counter-raids.

### Groton Burns (March 1676)

On **March 13, 1676**, it was Groton's turn.

Native American forces attacked the town. Buildings burned. Residents fled or died. The town Matthias had known since he was fifteen—where his father was an original proprietor, where his family had land and status—went up in flames.

What did Matthias do that day? Fight to defend the town? Help evacuate women and children? Watch his home burn?

We don't know the details. But we know he survived.

Most Groton residents fled to safer towns and stayed away for years. But eventually, survivors like the Farnsworths returned to rebuild.

### The War's End (1678)

King Philip's War officially ended in 1678, though violence continued sporadically. The Indigenous coalition was defeated. King Philip himself was killed, his head displayed on a pike in Plymouth for twenty years.

The cost was staggering:
- Approximately 600 English colonists dead (about 5% of the total population)
- Approximately 3,000 Native Americans dead (about 40% of Native population in the region)
- Twelve of ninety Puritan towns destroyed
- English supremacy in southern New England secured

Matthias Jr., now about twenty-nine years old, had spent three years at war. He had seen things no human should see. He had done things survival required.

And now he needed to rebuild a life.

## Marriage and Family

### Sarah Nutting

On **March 1, 1681**, Matthias married **Sarah Nutting**.

**Sarah's Background**:
- Born March 29, 1663, in Groton
- Daughter of John Nutting and Sarah Eggleston
- Another founding Groton family
- Age 17 at marriage (Matthias was about 32)

The fifteen-year age gap was not unusual, especially for a man marrying after years of war. Many men Matthias's age had delayed marriage during the war years. Many potential brides had been killed or had fled the region.

Sarah was young, but frontier women matured quickly. At seventeen, she was ready to run a household, bear children, endure hardships.

### Building a Life Together

Matthias and Sarah set about building the life the war had delayed:

- **Farming**: Matthias worked land in Groton, growing crops to feed his family and perhaps sell surplus
- **Home**: Building and maintaining a house (probably a simple structure, but theirs)
- **Children**: Starting a family to replace the devastation of war
- **Community**: Helping rebuild Groton, attending church, participating in town governance

This was the dream—after years of violence, the simple pleasures of family, farm, and community.

### Six Children in Twelve Years

Matthias and Sarah had at least six children:

1. **Joseph Farnsworth** (born January 7, 1682)
   - Less than a year after marriage
   - The firstborn

2. **Ebenezer Farnsworth** (date unknown)

3. **[Name unknown]** (possibly others)

4. **Josiah Farnsworth** (born February 24, 1687)

5. **[Name unknown]** (possibly others)

6. **Matthias Farnsworth III** (born August 6, 1690)
   - The youngest known son
   - Would later become Claude-Mathias Phaneuf

Six children in less than ten years of marriage—the typical pattern for colonial families. Sarah was likely pregnant or nursing for most of the 1680s.

This was the rhythm of frontier life: endless cycles of pregnancy, birth, nursing, weaning, and pregnancy again. Many women bore ten, twelve, or more children, though not all survived.

## The Garrison Assignment

### March 17, 1692

On **March 17, 1692**, Matthias Farnsworth was **assigned to the Farnsworth garrison** in Groton, organized for defense against potential Indian raids.

This assignment came at a significant moment:
- **His father had died** in January 1689, just three years earlier
- **King William's War** had begun in 1688, bringing renewed conflict
- **The frontier was again dangerous**

### What a Garrison Meant

A **garrison** was a fortified house where families could shelter during attacks. The Farnsworth garrison suggests:

- **It was likely his father's former property**, now Matthias's responsibility as the eldest surviving son
- **It was substantial enough to serve as a defensive structure** with thick walls, gun ports, and space for multiple families
- **Matthias was responsible for** maintaining it, defending it, and providing shelter to neighbors during alarms

Being assigned to a garrison was both:
- **An honor**: Recognition of reliability and property
- **A burden**: Constant responsibility and danger

Matthias now had to:
- Maintain weapons and ammunition
- Stand watch rotations
- Respond to alarms day or night
- Defend not just his family but his neighbors
- Keep the garrison supplied and ready

This was in addition to farming, raising six young children, and all the other demands of frontier life.

### The Weight of Responsibility

Consider what Matthias carried in early 1692:
- **Six children**, the youngest just two years old
- **A wife** dependent on him
- **A farm** to work
- **A garrison** to maintain and defend
- **Memories** of King Philip's War and the destruction of Groton
- **Awareness** that war had returned and danger was constant

He was thirty-two years old and responsible for everything.

---

## Death in the Indian Wars

### King William's War (1688-1697)

The relative peace after King Philip's War didn't last. By 1688, **King William's War** (part of the larger European War of the Grand Alliance) brought renewed violence to New England.

French forces from Canada, allied with Abenaki and other Indigenous nations, raided English frontier settlements. The pattern of King Philip's War resumed:
- Raids on isolated farms and small towns
- Ambushes of work parties in fields
- Garrison houses under siege
- Constant vigilance required

Groton, as a frontier town, was again vulnerable.

### 1693: The Year of His Death

Sometime in 1693, Matthias Farnsworth Jr. died.

We don't have the exact date or circumstances, but we know:
- An **inventory of his estate** was taken by his widow Sarah on **November 8, 1693**
- The timing (during active warfare) and context (his garrison assignment) suggest **death in combat or during a raid**
- He probably died fighting—defending his garrison, responding to an alarm, or protecting his community

Did he die:
- In a raid on Groton itself?
- Defending another settlement?
- In an ambush while working his fields?
- On a military expedition against enemy forces?

We'll never know. But he died as he had lived—fighting to protect what was his.

### What He Left Behind

Matthias Farnsworth Jr.'s death in 1693 left:

**Sarah**: A thirty-year-old widow with six children to raise alone on the frontier

**Joseph**: Age 11
**Ebenezer**: Age unknown
**Josiah**: Age 6
**Matthias III**: Age 3—too young to remember his father

**Other children**: Names and ages unknown

Sarah faced impossible choices:
- How to run the farm without her husband?
- How to protect her children?
- How to survive economically?
- Whether to remarry (necessary for survival)?
- How to preserve her husband's legacy?

### The Widow's Remarriage

On **December 16, 1698**—five years after Matthias's death—Sarah married **John Stone** in Concord, Massachusetts.

This was necessary. A widow with young children couldn't survive alone on the frontier. She needed:
- A man to work the farm
- Protection
- Economic support
- Help raising the children

By remarrying, Sarah ensured her children's survival. But it meant:
- Young Matthias III grew up with a stepfather, not his biological father
- The Farnsworth children lived in Stone's household
- Matthias Jr.'s direct influence on his children ended with his death

## The Tragic Pattern

### Father and Son

The parallel is heartbreaking:

**Matthias Farnsworth Jr.**:
- Survived King Philip's War in his twenties
- Married and had six children in his thirties
- Died fighting in his forties
- Killed by the same colonial conflicts that had defined his entire life

**Matthias Farnsworth III** (his son):
- Lost his father to the Indian wars at age 3
- Captured by Abenaki warriors at age 14
- Became Claude-Mathias Phaneuf—taken from one world into another

Both were casualties of the same brutal colonial wars. The father died fighting. The son was taken, transformed, and assimilated.

### The Question of Memory

Matthias III was only three years old when his father died. He would have had:
- No real memories of him
- Only stories from his mother and older brothers
- A stepfather (John Stone) as the father figure he actually knew
- A sense of loss and absence where a father should have been

When fourteen-year-old Matthias was captured in 1704, what did he remember of Groton?
- His father's death?
- The constant fear?
- The violence that had killed his father and threatened everyone?
- The rigid Puritan discipline?
- The stepfamily situation?

Perhaps his choice to eventually stay in New France rather than return to Massachusetts becomes more understandable. What did Massachusetts hold for him except painful memories and a stepfather's household?

## Legacy

### A Brief Life

Matthias Farnsworth Jr. lived only about forty-four years:
- Born c. 1649 (probably in England)
- Came to New England as an infant
- Grew up in Lynn and Groton
- Survived King Philip's War
- Married at 32
- Had six children
- Assigned to defend the Farnsworth garrison
- Died fighting at about 44

It was a life of constant struggle—war, rebuilding, raising a family, war again, death.

But in those forty-four years, he:
- Helped rebuild Groton after its destruction
- Married and created a family
- Raised children who would carry on his name
- Defended his community to the death

### Father of the Phaneuf Line

Through his youngest son's transformation, Matthias Farnsworth Jr. became:
- The father of Claude-Mathias Phaneuf
- The grandfather of twelve Phaneuf children
- An ancestor of all Phaneufs in North America

His English, Puritan bloodline entered French, Catholic Quebec through his son's capture and conversion.

### What He Never Knew

Matthias died in 1693. He never knew that:
- His youngest son would be captured eleven years later
- His son would be ransomed by a French priest
- His son would convert to Catholicism
- His son would take a French name and identity
- His son would marry a French woman
- His Puritan son would raise Catholic, French-speaking children
- His bloodline would establish an entirely new family name in New France

If he had lived, would he have:
- Fought harder to ransom Matthias III back from captivity?
- Given his son stronger ties to Groton?
- Changed the course of history?

We'll never know. But his death in 1693 shaped the conditions that enabled his son's transformation in 1704.

## Why His Story Matters

Matthias Farnsworth Jr.'s story matters because:

**He represents sacrifice**: Dying to defend his community and family

**He shows the human cost of colonial wars**: A husband and father killed, a widow and orphans left behind

**He connects two worlds**: English and French, Protestant and Catholic, Massachusetts and Quebec—all through his son's transformation

**He reminds us that history is personal**: Behind every statistic of colonial warfare is a Matthias Farnsworth—a real person with a family, dreams, fears, and a life cut short

**He shaped what came after**: His death created the conditions for his son's capture and transformation

When you think of your Phaneuf ancestors, remember that the line began with sacrifice—an English farmer who died defending his frontier home, leaving a three-year-old son who would grow up to bridge two worlds.
