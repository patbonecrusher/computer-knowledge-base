# Anne Couvent - The Immigrant Who Carried the Blood of Kings (1636)

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/anne-couvent|Anne Couvent Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

Imagine: A young woman, perhaps 32 years old, stands on the deck of a ship approaching Quebec in the summer of 1636. She holds the hands of two small boys—Jean, about 11, and Mathieu, about 8. Her husband Philippe stands beside her. They're leaving everything familiar behind: France, Picardy, family, comfort.

They're sailing toward a tiny settlement of perhaps 500 souls clinging to the edge of a vast wilderness. Harsh winters. Dangerous. Primitive. A gamble.

What the passengers on that ship didn't know—what Anne herself almost certainly didn't know—was that this seemingly ordinary woman carried something extraordinary in her veins.

**The blood of seven royal houses.**

In Anne Couvent's ancestry flowed the lineages of:
- The Kings of France
- The Kings of England
- The Holy Roman Emperors
- The Crusader Kings of Jerusalem
- The Kings of Navarre, Castile, and Constantinople
- And reaching back through the centuries: William the Conqueror and Charlemagne himself

For over 370 years, this secret remained hidden. Anne died in 1675, her royal connections unknown. Generation after generation of her descendants lived and died never knowing they carried royal blood.

Until 2007, when two genealogists finally uncovered the truth.

This is the story of Anne Couvent—immigrant, widow, survivor—and the seven crowns hidden in her family tree.

---

## Part I: The Hidden Princess of Picardy

### A Noblewoman in Disguise?

Anne Couvent was born around 1601-1604 in **Estrées** (now called Cœuvres-et-Valsery), a small town in the **Aisne department of Picardy**, northern France.

Her father: **Guillaume Convent dit Estrée** (probably minor gentry or bourgeoisie)
Her mother: **Antoinette de Longueval** (born around 1582)

On the surface, this appears to be an ordinary French family. Guillaume Convent wasn't titled nobility. The family wasn't wealthy or famous. They lived in Picardy, not at the royal court in Paris.

But Anne's mother—**Antoinette de Longueval**—that's where the story gets interesting.

### The Longueval Connection

Antoinette de Longueval was born in **Soissons, Picardy** around 1582. Her parents were:
- **Charles de Longueval**, Ecuyer Seigneur de Sivry et de Vuallicourt
- **Louise de Joyeuse**, Dame of Sivry

The Longueval family was French **nobility**—not the highest ranks, but genuine aristocracy with land, titles, and connections.

But it's Anne's **grandmother**—Louise de Joyeuse—where everything changes.

### The House of Joyeuse

The **Joyeuse family** was significant French nobility. They were:
- Lords (later Dukes) of Joyeuse in southern France
- Connected to the royal court
- Married into other noble families for generations
- Participants in the religious and political struggles of 16th century France

Louise de Joyeuse connected the Longueval family to a web of noble lineages stretching across Europe—lineages that reached back, generation by generation, to the crowned heads of Christendom.

### The Royal Secret

Here's the remarkable thing: **Anne probably didn't know**.

In the early 1600s, genealogy wasn't what it is today. There were no comprehensive records. Families told stories, but detailed documentation of ancestry beyond a few generations was rare except among the highest nobility.

Anne knew her mother was "de Longueval"—a noble name. She probably knew her grandmother was "de Joyeuse"—another noble name. But did she know those names connected, fifteen generations back, to Henry III of England? To Louis VIII of France? To Charlemagne?

Almost certainly not.

She was the daughter of Guillaume Convent, who wasn't titled. Her family wasn't wealthy. They lived in a provincial town. Whatever noble blood flowed in her veins had been diluted by generations and obscured by female lines.

The **blood of kings ran through her unrecognized**.

## Part II: Seven Crowns

In 2007, French-Canadian genealogists **Roland-Yves Gagné** and **Laurent Kokanosky** published groundbreaking research that changed everything.

Their article—"Les origines de Philippe Amiot (Hameau), de son épouse Anne Couvent et de leur neveu Toussaint Ledran"—appeared in *Mémoires de la Société généalogique canadienne-française* and presented meticulous documentation of Anne Couvent's ancestry.

What they discovered was astonishing: Anne descended from **seven different royal houses**.

Let's trace each crown.

### Crown #1: England - Henry III (1207-1272)

**Henry III, King of England**
Reigned: 1216-1272

The grandson of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine. Son of King John (who signed the Magna Carta). Father of Edward I "Longshanks." Brother-in-law to Louis IX of France (Saint Louis).

Henry III ruled England for 56 years—one of the longest reigns in English history.

**How Anne Connected to Henry III:**

The path runs through 16 generations:

Anne Couvent
↑ Antoinette de Longueval
↑ Louise de Joyeuse
↑ Jean de Joyeuse
↑ François de Joyeuse
↑ Marguerite de Barbançon
↑ François de Barbançon
↑ Robert de Sarrebruche
↑ Marie de Chateauvillain
↑ Jeanne de Grancey
↑ Yolande de Bar
↑ Marie de Dampierre
↑ Marie d'Artois
↑ Blanche d'Artois
↑ Beatrice de Dreux
↑ **Henry III, King of England**

Each step is documented. Each marriage recorded. The chain is unbroken.

### Crown #2: France - Louis VIII (1187-1226)

**Louis VIII "the Lion", King of France**
Reigned: 1223-1226

Son of Philip II Augustus (who expanded French power dramatically). Father of **Saint Louis IX** (one of the most revered kings in French history).

Louis VIII invaded England in 1216 and was offered the English crown (though he never secured it). He conquered much of southern France from the Albigensian heretics.

Anne descended from the same royal house that produced Saint Louis—the king who built Sainte-Chapelle, who led two Crusades, who was canonized as a saint.

### Crown #3: Holy Roman Empire - Frederick I "Barbarossa" (c.1122-1190)

**Frederick I von Hohenstaufen "Barbarossa", Emperor**
Ruled: 1155-1190

One of the greatest Holy Roman Emperors. "Barbarossa" means "Red Beard" in Italian.

Frederick led:
- Multiple campaigns into Italy to assert Imperial authority
- The Third Crusade (where he drowned crossing a river in Anatolia)
- German expansion and consolidation

A legendary figure—so legendary that German folklore said he wasn't really dead, but sleeping in a mountain cave, waiting to return when Germany needed him.

Anne carried the blood of this legendary emperor.

### Crown #4: Jerusalem - Amalric I (1136-1174)

**Amalric I, King of Jerusalem**
Ruled: 1163-1174

A **Crusader King**.

Amalric ruled the Kingdom of Jerusalem—the fragile Latin Christian state established after the First Crusade. He:
- Fought constantly against Muslim forces
- Tried to conquer Egypt (unsuccessfully)
- Maintained the delicate balance of Crusader states
- Was brother to King Baldwin III

The Kingdom of Jerusalem would fall completely in 1291, but for a time, European kings ruled in the Holy Land. Anne descended from one of them.

### Crown #5: Navarre - Thibault I (1201-1253)

**Thibault I "the Posthumous", King of Navarre**
Ruled: 1234-1253

Also **Count of Champagne**—one of the wealthiest and most cultured regions of France.

Thibault was:
- A poet and troubadour (wrote songs and poems in French)
- A patron of arts and literature
- Called "posthumous" because his father died before his birth
- A major figure in French chivalric culture

Anne descended from a king who was also a poet—a reminder that medieval royalty wasn't all warfare and politics.

### Crown #6: Latin Constantinople - Baldwin I (1172-1205)

**Baldwin I, Emperor of Latin Constantinople**
Ruled: 1204-1205

In 1204, the Fourth Crusade went horribly wrong. Instead of attacking Muslim forces, the Crusaders **sacked Constantinople**, the greatest Christian city in the world, capital of the Byzantine Empire.

They established the **Latin Empire of Constantinople** and crowned **Baldwin I of Flanders** as Emperor.

It was a catastrophe—the Byzantine Empire never fully recovered. The Latin Empire was fragile and collapsed in 1261. But for a brief moment, western knights ruled the ancient capital of the Eastern Roman Empire.

Baldwin died in 1205, captured by Bulgarians after losing a battle.

Anne descended from this controversial emperor.

### Crown #7: Castile and León - Saint Fernando III (1199-1252)

**Saint Fernando III, King of Castile and León**
Ruled: Castile 1217-1252, León 1230-1252

Fernando **united** the kingdoms of Castile and León permanently—a crucial step in creating what would become Spain.

He led the **Reconquista**, reconquering much of Andalusia from the Moors:
- Captured Córdoba (1236)
- Captured Seville (1248)
- Pushed Muslim power to the edge of the Iberian Peninsula

He was so pious and successful that the Catholic Church **canonized him as a saint** in 1671.

Anne descended from a king who became a saint.

## Part III: Beyond the Seven Crowns

These seven royal lineages connect Anne to even more ancient royalty.

### William the Conqueror (c.1028-1087)

Through **Henry III of England**, Anne descended from **William the Conqueror**.

William was Henry III's 3rd great-grandfather.

**William I "the Conqueror"**:
- Duke of Normandy
- Invaded England in 1066 (Battle of Hastings)
- Became King of England
- Changed English history forever (Norman Conquest)
- Commissioned the Domesday Book

The most famous date in English history—**1066**—connects directly to Anne Couvent through documented lineage.

### Charlemagne (742-814)

Through multiple lines (Louis VIII of France, Henry III of England, Frederick Barbarossa), Anne descended from **Charlemagne**.

**Charles I "Charlemagne"** (meaning "Charles the Great"):
- King of the Franks
- Crowned **Holy Roman Emperor** by the Pope in 800 AD
- United most of Western Europe
- Called the "Father of Europe"
- Founded the Carolingian Empire

Charlemagne is the great unifying ancestor of European nobility. Almost all medieval royal families descended from him. But having a **documented** line to Charlemagne is rare and precious.

**The connection**: Charlemagne → Louis I "the Pious" → Charles II "the Bald" → multiple lines → eventually to Anne Couvent

### Even Further Back?

Through Charlemagne, lines theoretically extend to:
- Merovingian kings of the Franks
- Roman patrician families
- Visigothic royalty
- Some lines claim connection to ancient Roman emperors

These become more speculative the further back you go, but the documented line to Charlemagne (12th-13th century royalty) is solid.

## Part IV: The Voyage to New France (1636)

### Why Leave France?

In 1636, Anne Couvent and Philippe Amiot made the decision to leave France for New France.

Why?

**New France in 1636 was barely viable**:
- Quebec City founded in 1608 (only 28 years earlier)
- Total French population: perhaps 500-600 people
- Harsh winters (colder than anything in France)
- Constant threat from Iroquois confederacy
- Primitive conditions
- Fur trade economy (risky and unstable)

What would possess a family to leave France—even a family of modest means—for this?

Possibilities:
1. **Economic opportunity**: In France, land was scarce and controlled by nobility. In New France, land was available
2. **Adventure**: The New World held romantic appeal
3. **Religious mission**: Converting Indigenous peoples (though the Amiots weren't missionaries)
4. **Trouble in France**: Wars, debts, persecution, scandal?
5. **Connections**: Perhaps Anne's distant noble connections helped secure passage or land grants

We don't know for certain. But in 1636, they made the choice.

### The Journey

The Atlantic crossing in 1636 was:
- **Dangerous**: Storms, shipwreck, disease
- **Long**: 6-12 weeks depending on weather
- **Miserable**: Cramped, dark, wet below decks
- **One-way**: No real expectation of returning

Anne, Philippe, and their two sons (Jean about 11, Mathieu about 8) survived the crossing and arrived in Quebec in the summer of 1636.

Possibly **June 11, 1636** was their arrival date.

### A Third Son Born in the New World

On **August 26, 1636**, their third son **Charles** was baptized in Quebec.

He was **born in New France**—the first generation of the Amiot family in the New World.

Jean and Mathieu were born in France, carrying old world memories. Charles was entirely new world—born in the wilderness, never knowing France.

All three boys carried royal blood. None knew it.

## Part V: Widowhood and Survival

### Philippe's Death (1639)

Sometime before **September 1639**—probably in the summer of 1639—**Philippe Amiot died**.

The records don't tell us how:
- Disease? (Very common in early Quebec)
- Accident? (Frontier life was dangerous)
- Attack? (Iroquois raids were constant threats)
- Winter hardship? (Many died from cold and starvation)

He had survived in New France for only **3-4 years**.

This left Anne:
- A **widow at age 35-38**
- With **three young sons** (ages approximately 14, 11, and 3)
- In a **frontier settlement** with few resources
- **Thousands of miles from family** in France
- No way to return home

### Remarriage to Jacques Maheu (1639)

On **September 26, 1639**—perhaps only weeks or months after Philippe's death—Anne married **Jacques Maheu**.

This seems shockingly fast by modern standards. But in 17th century New France, it was **practical and necessary**:
- A woman alone with three children couldn't easily survive
- Men vastly outnumbered women in early Quebec
- Remarriage provided security, labor, support
- Widows were expected to remarry quickly

Anne and Jacques had two children together:
- **Marie Madeleine Maheu** (died in infancy - a common tragedy)
- **Jean Maheu** (survived)

Jacques Maheu died sometime before 1666.

### Third Marriage to Étienne Blanchon (1666)

In **1666**, Anne married for the third time—to **Étienne Blanchon**.

She was now **62-65 years old** (remarkable longevity for the era).

No children from this marriage (she was beyond childbearing years).

This marriage provided companionship and security in her old age.

### Death on Christmas Day (1675)

On **December 25, 1675**—Christmas Day—Anne Couvent died in Quebec.

She was approximately **71-74 years old**.

She had lived in New France for **39 years**.

She had:
- Survived the Atlantic crossing
- Buried two husbands (Philippe and Jacques)
- Raised four sons to adulthood
- Endured harsh winters, frontier hardships, disease, danger
- Built a new life in a new world

And she died never knowing she carried the blood of seven royal houses.

## Part VI: The Secret Revealed (2007)

### 370 Years of Silence

From Anne's death in 1675 to 2007, her royal connections were **completely unknown**.

Generation after generation of her descendants:
- Married
- Had children
- Lived their lives
- Died

Never knowing they carried royal blood.

The **Amiot** family became prominent in Quebec. The **Maheu** family spread throughout New France. But none knew their connection to the Plantagenets, the Capetians, the Hohenstaufens, the Crusader Kings.

### Why Was It Hidden?

Several reasons:

**1. Female Lines Are Harder to Trace**

Royal descent through **mothers** is more easily lost:
- Women changed surnames when they married
- Noble lineages were tracked through fathers
- Women's ancestry was often not recorded in detail

Anne's royal blood came through her **mother** (Antoinette de Longueval), who got it from her **mother** (Louise de Joyeuse).

Female → Female → Female lineage is genealogically challenging.

**2. No Noble Status in New France**

The Amiot family in Quebec were **habitants**—farmers, not nobles.

They had no titles, no coats of arms, no obvious connection to royalty. Without some external clue (a family story, a document, a coat of arms), how would anyone know to look for royal ancestry?

**3. Distant Connections**

Anne's royal ancestors were **12-16 generations** in the past by the time she lived.

- From Henry III (died 1272) to Anne (born ~1603) = ~14 generations
- From Charlemagne (died 814) to Anne = ~25 generations

These are **distant** connections. Finding them requires tracing hundreds of intervening generations through multiple countries.

**4. Skepticism and False Claims**

Many families have claimed royal descent. Most turned out to be:
- Wishful thinking
- Fabricated genealogies
- Mistakes
- Frauds

Professional genealogists are rightly skeptical of royal descent claims. Proving them requires **meticulous documentation**.

### The Breakthrough: Gagné and Kokanosky (2007)

In 2007, two French-Canadian genealogists—**Roland-Yves Gagné** and **Laurent Kokanosky**—published revolutionary research.

Their article title: **"Les origines de Philippe Amiot (Hameau), de son épouse Anne Couvent et de leur neveu Toussaint Ledran"**

Published in: ***Mémoires de la Société généalogique canadienne-française*** volume 58, number 1, issue 251 (Spring 2007), pages 17-58.

**What they did:**

1. **Traced Anne's maternal line** from Anne → Antoinette de Longueval → Louise de Joyeuse

2. **Connected Joyeuse to documented noble families** through meticulous research in:
   - French archives
   - Noble genealogies
   - Medieval documents
   - Published research on European royalty
   - A crucial **1733 notarial document** (more on this below)

3. **Documented each generation** with sources:
   - Birth records
   - Marriage records
   - Contemporary chronicles
   - Noble lineage books

4. **Traced multiple royal lines** showing that Louise de Joyeuse connected to not just one, but **seven** different royal houses

5. **Published with full documentation** in a peer-reviewed genealogical journal

This is the **gold standard** for proving royal descent.

### But There's a Controversy: The "Black Sheep" Problem

The royal lineages depend on one crucial link: **François de Joyeuse**, who lived around 1520-1597.

According to Gagné and Kokanosky's research:
- François was the son of **Robert de Joyeuse** (Comte de Grandpré) and **Marguerite de Barbançon**
- François married and had a son Jean
- Jean had a daughter Louise
- Louise married Charles de Longueval (Anne Couvent's great-grandparents)

**The problem**: François de Joyeuse is **not mentioned** in the classical French genealogies.

**Père Anselme** (the definitive source on French nobility, published 1674-1733) lists the children of Robert de Joyeuse—but doesn't include François.

**La Chesnaye-Desbois** (another authoritative genealogical reference) also omits François from Robert's children.

Why?

### The 1733 Document: "His Name and His Sword"

The key evidence comes from a **notarial act dated March 20, 1733**—inscribed by Jean-Baptiste de Joyeuse to notary Pierre Hanonnet of Verdun.

This document tells a dramatic story:

**François de Joyeuse was the "black sheep" of his family.**

For his **first marriage**, François rebelled against his parents' wishes and married **a woman of very inferior social background** (her name is unknown—she wasn't noble).

This was a scandal for the noble de Joyeuse family.

François's father—**Robert de Joyeuse**, described as "a physically powerful and violent man"—was furious. He **disowned his son**, leaving him only:

**"Son nom et son épée"** ("His name and his sword")

Nothing else. No inheritance. No lands. No titles. No money.

Just his name (he was still a Joyeuse) and his sword (the symbol of nobility).

**This explains why François doesn't appear in the classical genealogies**: He was written out of the family. Disowned. Erased from the record.

Père Anselme and La Chesnaye-Desbois, compiling their noble genealogies, likely followed the "official" family records—which had removed François for his disgrace.

### The Genealogical Debate

This creates a **major controversy** in the genealogical community:

**Skeptics (led by Charles Cawley of the Foundation for Medieval Genealogy)** argue:
- The 1733 document is **164 years after** François's death
- It's hearsay from a descendant (Jean-Baptiste de Joyeuse) claiming connection to the disowned line
- No contemporary sources mention François as Robert's son
- We're asked to trust a single 18th century document contradicting published genealogies
- The incentive to claim noble ancestry is obvious

**Supporters (Gagné, Kokanosky, and John P. DuLong)** counter:
- The 1733 document is a **notarial act**—a legal document, not just family tradition
- It was attested before a notary (Pierre Hanonnet), giving it legal weight
- The story **explains** why François is missing from classical genealogies (he was disowned)
- They found corroborating evidence in other documents
- DuLong published a rebuttal in 2023: *"The Joyeuse Ancestry of the Couvents"*

**The current consensus**:
- Most French-Canadian genealogists **accept** the connection (Gagné and Kokanosky are highly respected)
- Some medieval genealogists remain **skeptical** (wanting more contemporary evidence)
- The royal lineages stand—but with an asterisk: "depends on the François de Joyeuse connection"

### Why This Matters

If François de Joyeuse was **not** Robert and Marguerite's son, then:
- Anne Couvent still has noble ancestry (through other lines)
- But the connections to **Henry III, Louis VIII, and the other six royal houses** would be broken
- Anne would not be a "gateway ancestor" to recent European royalty

If François **was** Robert and Marguerite's son (as the 1733 document claims), then:
- The seven royal lineages are valid
- Anne Couvent descends from kings
- Thousands of her descendants carry documented royal blood

**Most genealogists accept the connection**, based on:
- The 1733 notarial document
- Gagné and Kokanosky's thorough research
- DuLong's 2023 rebuttal
- The logical explanation (disownment) for François's absence from classical sources

But honest researchers acknowledge: **This link has some uncertainty.**

### The Impact

Suddenly, thousands of descendants of Anne Couvent realized they carried royal blood.

The genealogical community recognized a new **gateway ancestor**:

**Gateway Ancestor** = Someone who provides documented royal connections for large numbers of descendants

Anne joined only two other women as royal gateways for French-Canadians:
1. Catherine de Baillon (Fille du Roi, 1669)
2. Jeanne Le Marchand (married Jean Le Neuf de la Poterie)
3. **Anne Couvent** (immigrant 1636)

And Anne's connections are the **most recent** and thus the most extensively documented.

## Part VII: What This Means

### For Anne's Descendants

If you descend from Anne Couvent (through any of her sons), you carry:

**The blood of kings:**
- Plantagenet (England)
- Capetian (France)
- Hohenstaufen (Holy Roman Empire)
- Crusader dynasties (Jerusalem, Navarre, Constantinople, Castile)
- Norman (William the Conqueror)
- Carolingian (Charlemagne)

**This doesn't make you nobility.**

Nobility is a legal status that descends through specific rules (usually male-line primogeniture). You don't inherit titles or coats of arms through Anne Couvent.

**But it is historically real.**

The blood connection is genuine. The documented lineages prove it. You are, in a very literal genetic sense, descended from royalty.

### The Strangeness of Royal Descent

Here's something to think about:

**How many descendants did Charlemagne have?**

Charlemagne died in 814 AD. That's over **1,200 years ago**.

If we assume:
- 25 years per generation
- Average of 3 children per generation who survived to have children
- 48 generations from Charlemagne to today

The math becomes absurd. The number of theoretical descendants exceeds the total human population many times over.

**The truth**: Almost **everyone of European descent** probably descends from Charlemagne.

The difference is **documentation**.

Most people's connection to Charlemagne is lost in the mists of time. We can theorize it exists, but we can't prove it.

**Anne Couvent's descendants can prove it.**

That's what makes her special. Not that she uniquely carried royal blood (many did), but that her royal blood can be **documented**.

### The Mystery of Royal Blood

There's something poignant about Anne's story.

She lived and died in Quebec, never knowing she descended from kings. She scrubbed floors, cooked meals, raised children, buried husbands, endured hardship.

No crown. No throne. No palace.

But in her veins flowed the blood of:
- Henry Plantagenet, who ruled England
- Louis Capet, who ruled France
- Frederick Hohenstaufen, who ruled the Holy Roman Empire
- The warriors who conquered Jerusalem
- The kings who reconquered Spain
- The poets who sang of chivalry
- The emperor who united Europe

**Blood doesn't determine destiny.**

Anne's royal ancestors commanded armies, signed treaties, built cathedrals, led crusades. Anne washed laundry in Quebec.

But perhaps that's the point: **The blood of kings flows in ordinary people.**

The princes and paupers are all connected. The thrones of Europe and the farms of Quebec share the same ancestors.

### What Would Anne Think?

If you could go back to 1636 and tell Anne Couvent—standing on that ship deck approaching Quebec—that she descended from seven royal houses...

Would she believe you?

Would she care?

Would it change her life?

Maybe she'd laugh. Maybe she'd be proud. Maybe she'd shrug and say, "Well, I still need to get these boys fed and find a place to live."

Royal blood doesn't keep you warm in a Quebec winter. It doesn't plant crops or raise children. It doesn't matter when your husband dies and you're alone in a wilderness.

**But it does mean something.**

It means that in the long chain of history, from Charlemagne through a thousand years to Anne Couvent to you, something endured.

Not power. Not wealth. Not status.

But **blood**. **Family**. **Connection**.

You are part of that chain.

## Part VIII: The Other Gateway: Charlotte Couvent and Toussaint Ledran

Anne wasn't the only Couvent sister to come to New France.

Her sister **Charlotte Couvent** married **Louis Ledran** in France.

Their son **Toussaint Ledran** immigrated to New France as well.

This means:
- Anyone who descends from **Anne Couvent** has the seven royal lineages
- Anyone who descends from **Charlotte Couvent** (through Toussaint Ledran) **also** has the seven royal lineages

The same mother (Antoinette de Longueval) gave both sisters the same royal blood.

**Toussaint Ledran** was Anne's nephew. His descendants are as royal as Anne's. All the crowns that connected through Anne also connected through Charlotte.

This **doubles** the number of people who descend from the Longueval/Joyeuse royal gateway.
