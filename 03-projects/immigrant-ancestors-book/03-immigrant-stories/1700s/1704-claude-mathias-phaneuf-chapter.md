# Claude-Mathias Phaneuf (Born Matthias Farnsworth III) - 1704

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/claude-mathias-phaneuf|Claude-Mathias Phaneuf Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

August 11, 1704. A warm summer day in Groton, Massachusetts. Fourteen-year-old Matthias Farnsworth III worked alongside other men in the fields, harvesting crops under the New England sun. It was hard work, but necessary work—the kind that filled the barns and kept families fed through the long winter ahead.

He didn't hear them coming.

Twenty Abenaki warriors, allied with the French in Queen Anne's War, swept through the fields. Within moments, young Matthias was a captive, bound and being marched north toward a world utterly foreign to everything he knew. He would never see Massachusetts again.

But this wasn't the end of Matthias Farnsworth's story. It was the beginning of Claude-Mathias Phaneuf's.

This is the story of a boy stolen from one world and reborn in another—and of how every person bearing the surname Phaneuf today descends from that terrified fourteen-year-old being dragged north through the wilderness in the summer of 1704.

---

## Life in Puritan New England

### Groton, Massachusetts

Matthias Farnsworth III was born on August 6, 1690, in Groton, a frontier town in Middlesex County, Massachusetts. Groton sat on the edge of English settlement—beyond it lay the vast wilderness, and beyond that, New France.

His parents, Matthias Farnsworth II and Sarah Nutting, were Puritans, part of the religious movement that had established the Massachusetts Bay Colony seeking freedom to practice their strict Protestant faith. The Farnsworths were an established English family, though not wealthy or particularly prominent.

Life in Groton was hard and dangerous. The town had been attacked and destroyed during King Philip's War in 1676—just fourteen years before Matthias's birth—and had only recently been resettled. Everyone knew that the wilderness held dangers: harsh weather, difficult farming, wild animals, and most fearfully, raids.

### Growing Up on the Frontier

Young Matthias grew up in a world of:

- **Puritan Faith**: Church services lasting hours. Strict Sabbath observance. The Bible as the center of life. Education focused on reading Scripture. A worldview that saw Catholics as dangerous papists and Indigenous peoples as either souls to be saved or enemies to be feared.

- **Constant Work**: Farming, hunting, building, repairing. Even fourteen-year-old boys were expected to work like men. The family's survival depended on everyone's labor.

- **Ever-Present Danger**: Frontier settlements like Groton lived in the shadow of conflict. The colonial wars between English and French forces, each allied with different Indigenous nations, meant raids could come at any time.

Matthias's childhood was shaped by these realities. He learned to read the Bible, to work the land, to be wary, to survive.

### The Shadow of War

By 1704, war had returned. **Queen Anne's War** (1702-1713)—known in Europe as the War of Spanish Succession—had brought renewed conflict between French Canada and English New England.

French colonial authorities encouraged their Indigenous allies, particularly the Abenaki, to raid English settlements. These raids served multiple purposes:
- Weaken English expansion
- Capture prisoners for ransom
- Take captives who might be assimilated into French or Indigenous society
- Instill terror and discourage settlement

Just six months before Matthias's capture, in February 1704, the famous **Deerfield Massacre** had shocked all of New England. Abenaki and French forces attacked Deerfield, Massachusetts, killing 56 people and taking 112 captive on a brutal winter march to Canada.

Groton knew it could be next.

---

## The Raid

### August 11, 1704

It was harvest time. The fields around Groton were heavy with crops that needed bringing in before autumn. Men and boys worked together, probably keeping weapons nearby but focused on the urgent task of gathering food for winter.

Around twenty Abenaki warriors, allied with the French, moved silently through the forests toward Groton. They knew exactly what they were doing—these raids were carefully planned operations, not random attacks.

When they struck the fields where Matthias and others worked, it was swift and overwhelming. There was no time to run, no time to reach weapons, no possibility of resistance.

Fourteen-year-old Matthias Farnsworth III became a prisoner of war.

### The Captives

Matthias wasn't alone. The warriors took multiple captives—men, women, and children were all valuable, either for ransom or for adoption into Indigenous communities or sale to French colonists.

The captives were immediately bound and prepared for the long, brutal journey north. Those who couldn't keep up—the very old, the very young, the sick or injured—were often killed. Captivity was not for the weak.

For Matthias, the world he had known—his family, his home, his language, his faith, his identity as an English boy—was suddenly, violently torn away.

---

## The Forced March North

### Into the Wilderness

The journey from Groton, Massachusetts to Montreal covered roughly 250 miles of wilderness. For captives, it was a nightmare ordeal:

- **The Pace**: Forced marches covering many miles per day, with little rest
- **The Terrain**: Through forests, across rivers, over mountains
- **The Provisions**: Minimal food, whatever could be hunted or gathered
- **The Fear**: Not knowing your fate, watching for any sign of what would happen
- **The Isolation**: Cut off from everything familiar, unable to communicate effectively

But Matthias was young and strong. He survived when others didn't. He kept up. He endured.

As they traveled north, they moved from English territory into the contested borderlands, and finally into New France—a world completely alien to a Puritan boy from Massachusetts.

### Arrival at Sault-au-Récollet

The captives were brought to **Sault-au-Récollet**, a mission near Montreal where Christianized Indigenous peoples lived under French Jesuit and Sulpician oversight. This was a common destination for English captives.

Here, Matthias entered a liminal world—neither fully Indigenous nor French, a place where English captives, Indigenous converts, French missionaries, and traders all intersected.

He lived among the Abenaki from late summer 1704 through all of 1705 and into early 1706. Over a year. For a teenage boy, this was a formative period. He learned:

- Survival in a completely different environment
- Indigenous ways and language (at least some)
- French language (the lingua franca of the mission)
- Catholic practices (whether willingly or not, he was exposed to them daily)
- That the French and Indigenous people weren't the demons his Puritan upbringing had taught

### The Psychology of Captivity

What does a fourteen-year-old boy think during such an ordeal?

Did he dream of escape? Of rescue? Of his family?

Did he resist or gradually adapt?

Did he cling to his Puritan faith or find it failing him in this new world?

We don't know his inner thoughts, but we know this: when opportunity came for him to return to English society, he chose not to take it. Something fundamental changed during those months in captivity.

---

## Redemption and Rebirth

### François Vachon de Belmont

In late 1705 or early 1706, a powerful figure took interest in young Matthias: **François Vachon de Belmont** (1645-1732), a Sulpician priest who was:

- Director of the Sulpician Order in New France
- Superior of the Seminary of Montreal
- Lord of the Island of Montreal (seigneur)
- One of the most influential religious and political figures in the colony

Why did Belmont take interest in this English captive? Perhaps:
- Christian charity and a desire to save a soul from "heresy"
- Recognition of intelligence or potential in the young man
- A belief that converted English captives could become valuable members of French society
- Simply the opportunity to demonstrate French civilization and Catholicism's superiority

Whatever his motivation, Belmont paid the ransom to "redeem him from the Indians who had him in slavery."

### The Transaction

The exchange of captives was a complex economy in colonial North America:

- **Indigenous captors** could sell or ransom prisoners
- **French authorities** could buy English captives to convert them or trade them back to English colonies
- **English colonies** would sometimes pay ransoms for the return of their people
- **Individual families** would desperately try to raise funds to redeem loved ones

Belmont paid the price, and Matthias Farnsworth III passed from Indigenous custody into French Catholic society.

But not as Matthias Farnsworth. That identity was about to die.

### Baptism and Rebirth

**January 10, 1706**. Notre-Dame Basilica, Montreal. The heart of French Catholic power in New France.

Matthias Farnsworth III was baptized—or more accurately, conditionally re-baptized (his Puritan baptism as an infant would not have been recognized by the Catholic Church). The ceremony transformed him:

**New Name**: Mathias-Claude Farneth
- "Mathias" retained his birth name
- "Claude" honored his godfather
- "Farneth" was the first French rendering of "Farnsworth"

**Godfather**: **Claude de Ramezay** (1659-1724)
- Governor of Montreal
- One of the most powerful men in New France
- Future Governor General of New France (1714-1716)

**Godmother**: **Elisabeth Souart**
- Wife of Charles le Moyne de Longueuil, Baron de Longueuil
- Part of one of the most prominent seigneurial families in New France

These were not ordinary godparents. For a former English captive to have the Governor of Montreal and a Baroness as sponsors was extraordinary. It suggests:

- Belmont's high hopes for the young convert
- The prestige and propaganda value of converting an English captive
- Perhaps genuine affection and recognition of Matthias's character or potential
- The French colonial elite's investment in creating a new French identity for him

In that moment, standing in Notre-Dame with the Governor and a Baroness as his spiritual parents, Matthias Farnsworth ceased to exist.

Claude-Mathias was born.

---

## Becoming French

### A New World, A New Identity

From 1706 onward, Claude-Mathias lived as a French subject and Catholic. He:

- Spoke French as his primary language (English faded)
- Practiced Catholicism (attended Mass, confession, communion)
- Dressed as the French did
- Adopted French customs and manners
- Integrated into French-Canadian society

For a teenager captured at fourteen and baptized at fifteen, this wasn't necessarily traumatic. Adolescence is when identity forms. The Puritan boy from Groton became the French-Canadian man in Quebec.

This wasn't uncommon. Many young captives, especially those taken before age sixteen or so, assimilated completely. The younger you were, the more plastic your identity, the easier the transformation.

### The Name Continues to Evolve

"Mathias-Claude Farneth" was just the beginning. Over the next fifty years, the surname went through a fascinating evolution:

1. **Farnsworth** (English original, 1690)
2. **Farneth** (baptism, 1706)
3. **Farnouth** (variations in records)
4. **Fanef** (marriage contract, 1713)
5. **Faneuf** (common spelling by 1720s-1730s)
6. **Faneuffe** (alternate spelling)
7. **Phaneffe** (moving toward standardization)
8. **Phaneuf** (standardized by mid-1800s)

This reflects how French-Canadian pronunciation reshaped the English surname. The Quebec French /fanœf/ sound—with its nasal vowels and simplified consonants—couldn't accommodate "Farnsworth." So the name gradually transformed to match how people actually said it.

By the time Claude-Mathias's grandchildren were born, "Farnsworth" was a distant memory. They were Phaneufs.

---

## Marriage and Family

### Finding a Wife

By 1713, Claude-Mathias was twenty-three years old. He had lived seven years in New France—half again as long as he'd lived as a conscious, aware person in Massachusetts. He was fully French now, in language, faith, and culture.

In September 1713, he signed a marriage contract with **Marie Catherine Charpentier**, daughter of his neighbor. She's sometimes listed with the additional name or dit name "Lapaille."

This was a normal French-Canadian marriage—a young man and woman from neighboring families, a marriage contract drawn up, families involved, the community's blessing.

No one seemed concerned that the groom had been born English. That was ancient history. He was Claude-Mathias Fanef, French subject, Catholic, member of the community.

The marriage took place, and Claude-Mathias and Catherine began building a life together.

### Twelve Children

Over the next years, Claude-Mathias and Catherine had **twelve children**. This was a large family even by the standards of New France, where big families were the norm.

Through these twelve children, Claude-Mathias became the **sole progenitor of the Phaneuf surname in North America**.

Think about that: every single person with the surname Phaneuf—in Quebec, in the United States, anywhere in the world—descends from this one man. From the terrified English boy captured in 1704.

This makes him unique among Quebec's founding families. Most surnames have multiple origins, multiple founding individuals. But Phaneuf? One source. One man. One story.

Their children grew up French-Canadian, fully integrated into Quebec society. They:
- Married into other French-Canadian families
- Spread the Phaneuf name throughout the St. Lawrence valley
- Had children of their own (lots of them)
- Established the family in multiple parishes and regions

Through the mathematics of genealogy—each child having children, those children having children—within a few generations, thousands of people descended from Claude-Mathias and Catherine.

---

## Life in French Canada

### St-Antoine-sur-Richelieu

Claude-Mathias and Catherine eventually settled in **St-Antoine-sur-Richelieu**, a parish along the Richelieu River southeast of Montreal. This was good farming land, part of the expanding French settlement along the river.

Here, Claude-Mathias lived as a **habitant**—a French-Canadian farmer. He:

- Worked the land (the same kind of agricultural labor he'd done as a boy in Groton)
- Raised his large family
- Participated in parish life (the Catholic church was the center of community)
- Was part of the fabric of French-Canadian society

To his neighbors, he was simply Claude-Mathias Phaneuf (or Fanef, or Faneuf—spelling was fluid). An ordinary man. A good Catholic. A hardworking farmer. A father of many children.

Few probably knew or remembered he'd been born English. Even fewer cared.

### A Long Life

Claude-Mathias lived to the remarkable age of **83**—exceptional for the 18th century. He died on **August 8, 1773** in St-Antoine-sur-Richelieu, just two days after his 83rd birthday.

He had lived:
- **14 years** as Matthias Farnsworth in Massachusetts (1690-1704)
- **1.5 years** as a captive (1704-1706)
- **67 years** as Claude-Mathias Phaneuf in Quebec (1706-1773)

He spent nearly five times as long as a Frenchman as he had as an Englishman.

By the time he died in 1773, he had been in Quebec for 69 years. He saw:
- The end of Queen Anne's War (1713)
- The growth and flourishing of New France
- The Seven Years' War (French and Indian War) (1754-1763)
- The British conquest of Quebec (1759-1760)
- The transition from French to British rule (after 1763)

He died a British subject (Quebec had been under British rule since 1763), speaking French, in a French-Catholic society, surrounded by his French-speaking Catholic grandchildren and great-grandchildren.

The English boy from Groton was long, long gone.

## Legacy

### The Sole Ancestor

Claude-Mathias Phaneuf holds a unique place in French-Canadian genealogy:

**Every single Phaneuf in the world descends from him.**

This includes:
- Thousands of Phaneufs in Quebec
- Phaneufs throughout Canada
- Phaneufs who migrated to the United States (especially New England and the Midwest)
- Phaneufs scattered worldwide through the French-Canadian diaspora

Some family names have multiple founding ancestors. Some have unclear origins. But Phaneuf? One man. One story. Crystal clear.

This has made the Phaneuf family history particularly well-documented and researched. Genealogists, family associations, and historians have thoroughly traced the family tree because there's one clear trunk from which every branch grows.

### A Bridge Between Two Worlds

Claude-Mathias represents something profound: the fluidity and violence of colonial identity.

**He was born English**: In a Puritan world that saw Catholics as dangerous, French as enemies, Indigenous peoples as threats.

**He was captured by Indigenous warriors**: Experienced firsthand the brutal realities of colonial warfare and the Indigenous-French alliance.

**He became French**: Converted to Catholicism, adopted French language and culture, married into French society, raised French children.

**His descendants include you**: The English Puritan heritage of the Farnsworths flows through Phaneuf veins, even as the family is thoroughly, completely French-Canadian.

### The New England Captive Narrative

Claude-Mathias is part of a larger historical phenomenon: the **New England captive narrative**.

During the colonial wars (particularly 1680s-1760s), thousands of English colonists were captured by French-allied Indigenous peoples. Many were ransomed back. Some were adopted into Indigenous communities. Some, like Claude-Mathias, were converted and integrated into French society.

Famous examples include:
- **Eunice Williams** (captured in the Deerfield Massacre, 1704, same year as Claude-Mathias) - She became Aongote, married a Mohawk man, and never returned to English society
- **Mary Jemison** - Captured 1755, lived her entire life among the Seneca
- **John Gyles** - Captured as a boy, lived years among the Maliseet

These captivity narratives fascinated both contemporary audiences and modern historians because they reveal:
- The porousness of cultural boundaries
- How identity is constructed and can be transformed
- The complex relationships between English, French, and Indigenous peoples
- The violence and trauma of colonialism
- The possibility of complete cultural transformation

Claude-Mathias didn't write a narrative (unlike some famous captives who published their stories). But his life speaks volumes.

### Why His Story Matters

Claude-Mathias Phaneuf's story matters because it's:

**A Story of Survival**: A fourteen-year-old boy survived capture, a brutal forced march, over a year of captivity, and built a new life from nothing.

**A Story of Transformation**: He went from English to French, Puritan to Catholic, Farnsworth to Phaneuf. Identity proved to be not fixed but fluid.

**A Story of Colonial Violence**: The raid on Groton, the captivity, the forced conversion—these weren't voluntary. They grew from the brutal conflicts that shaped North America.

**A Story of Adaptation**: Rather than remain paralyzed by trauma, Claude-Mathias adapted, integrated, and thrived. He lived to 83 and founded a family line that continues today.

**A Story of Cultural Complexity**: Colonial North America wasn't simply "French vs. English vs. Indigenous." It was a web of alliances, conflicts, transformations, and crossed boundaries. Claude-Mathias lived at those crossings.

**Your Story**: Because he is your ancestor. His choices, his survival, his transformation—they led to you.

## Historical Context: New England Captives and Queen Anne's War

### Queen Anne's War (1702-1713)

The conflict that made Claude-Mathias a captive was part of a larger war:

- **In Europe**: War of Spanish Succession—major European powers fighting over who would rule Spain
- **In North America**: Queen Anne's War—the second of the French and Indian Wars
- **The Pattern**: European conflicts spilled over into colonial wars in the Americas

### The French Strategy

French colonial authorities, outnumbered by English colonists, relied heavily on Indigenous allies. The strategy included:

- **Raids on English Settlements**: Disrupt expansion, weaken morale, capture prisoners
- **Indigenous Alliance**: The Abenaki, and other nations allied with the French
- **The Mission System**: Christianized Indigenous communities near Montreal served as bases
- **Prisoner Integration**: Convert and assimilate English captives, weakening English colonies

### The Abenaki

The warriors who captured Claude-Mathias were Abenaki, Indigenous peoples of what is now:
- Vermont
- New Hampshire
- Maine
- Parts of Quebec

The Abenaki had been driven from much of their territory by English expansion. Allied with the French, they:
- Conducted raids deep into New England
- Brought captives to missions near Montreal
- Played a crucial military role in defending New France

### The Captivity Experience

Historians estimate thousands of New Englanders were captured during the colonial wars. The experience varied:

**Immediate Aftermath**:
- Some captives were killed if they couldn't keep up on the march north
- Some were tortured (especially adult male soldiers)
- Most endured brutal forced marches of 200-300 miles

**In Captivity**:
- Some were adopted into Indigenous families (replacing dead relatives)
- Some were sold to French colonists as servants or for conversion
- Some were held for ransom
- Some were exchanged in prisoner swaps

**Long-Term Outcomes**:
- Many were eventually ransomed and returned to New England
- Some chose to stay in Indigenous communities
- Some, like Claude-Mathias, converted and integrated into French society
- A few managed to escape

### Why Did Some Captives Stay?

For those captured young (especially under 16), staying was common because:

**Psychological**: Identity forms in adolescence. A new language, new religion, new community could become "home"

**Social**: In Indigenous or French communities, they formed relationships, married, had children

**Practical**: Returning meant being viewed with suspicion (Had they really converted? Were they loyal?) and being "damaged goods" in rigid Puritan society

**Religious**: Some genuinely converted to Catholicism and couldn't return to Puritan communities

**Cultural**: French and especially Indigenous societies could be less restrictive than Puritan New England

For Claude-Mathias, all these factors likely played a role.

## Questions That Remain

Despite extensive research, mysteries remain about Claude-Mathias:

### About His Captivity
- **What happened on the march north?** Who else was captured? Did any escape or die?
- **What was his treatment among the Abenaki?** Was he destined for adoption, ransom, or something else?
- **Did he try to escape?** Or did he accept his fate?
- **What convinced Belmont to ransom him specifically?** Out of many captives, why this boy?

### About His Conversion
- **Was the conversion genuine or pragmatic?** Did he truly embrace Catholicism or simply do what was necessary to survive?
- **Did he maintain any English/Puritan identity privately?** Or did he fully become French?
- **How quickly did he learn French?** Was he fluent by the baptism?

### About His Family
- **Did he ever contact his birth family?** Did the Farnsworths in Massachusetts know what happened to him?
- **Did his children know his story?** Was he open about his English origins?
- **How did the name transformation happen?** Was it gradual, or did he actively choose new spellings?

### About His Later Life
- **What did he think as Quebec fell to the British (1759-1760)?** Did he feel any connection to his English heritage?
- **Did he tell his grandchildren stories of Massachusetts?** Or was that life completely buried?
- **What were his last thoughts (1773)?** Did he ever think about the fourteen-year-old boy in the Groton field?

These gaps frustrate but also intrigue. They remind us that even well-documented lives hold mysteries.
