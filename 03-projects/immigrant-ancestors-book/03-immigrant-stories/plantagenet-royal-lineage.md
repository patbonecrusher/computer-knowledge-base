# From Kings to Commoners: The Plantagenet Connection

## Introduction: A Gateway to Royalty

Through the Wolcott family line, our ancestry connects to one of the most powerful royal dynasties in European history: the **House of Plantagenet**, which ruled England for over three hundred years (1154-1485).

This connection runs through **Matilda Cornwall** (c.1388-1468), who married into the Wolcott family. Matilda's ancestry traces back through the Cornwall family to **Richard Plantagenet of Cornwall** (c.1252-1296), an illegitimate son of **Richard, 1st Earl of Cornwall** (1209-1272), who was himself the son of **King John of England** (1166-1216) and grandson of **Henry II Plantagenet** (1133-1189), the first Plantagenet king.

In genealogy, Matilda Cornwall represents a "**gateway ancestor**"—a person through whom millions of modern people can trace their descent from royalty. This chapter traces that connection and explores what it means.

## Part 2: Matilda Cornwall—The Gateway Ancestor

**Matilda Cornwall** (c.1388-1468) is the key figure connecting our family to royal lineage.

### Who Was Matilda?

Matilda was born around 1388 in Walcot, Shropshire, England, into the **Cornwall family** of that region. The Cornwalls were an established gentry family descended from the illegitimate royal line through Richard of Cornwall.

Around 1416, Matilda married into the **Wolcott family** (sometimes spelled Walcott, reflecting the same etymology). Through this marriage, she brought royal Plantagenet blood into the Wolcott line.

Her son **Roger Wolcott** (1425-1469) carried both the Wolcott name and the Cornwall/Plantagenet bloodline, passing it to all his descendants—including, eventually, the Wolcotts who would immigrate to America in the 1600s.

### Why Is She Called a "Gateway Ancestor"?

In genealogy, a "**gateway ancestor**" is someone who:
1. Has proven lineage to royalty or nobility
2. Has many descendants who became "commoners"
3. Serves as the connection point through which millions of modern people can trace royal ancestry

Matilda Cornwall is a classic gateway ancestor. Through her:
- Thousands of Wolcott descendants can claim Plantagenet ancestry
- The royal line dispersed into the general population
- Medieval kings became the ancestors of 21st-century ordinary people

## Part 3: The Cornwall Family—From Royalty to Gentry

The **Cornwall family** represents the transition from royalty to nobility to gentry to common folk over centuries.

### Richard Plantagenet of Cornwall (c.1252-1296)

The royal connection begins with **Richard of Cornwall** (c.1252-1296), also called **Richard de Cornwall** or **Richard de Cornubia**.

**His Parentage:**
- **Illegitimate son** of **Richard, 1st Earl of Cornwall** (1209-1272)
- His mother was **Joan de Valletort**
- Born outside marriage, but acknowledged by his royal father

**His Life:**
- Granted the **Manor of Thunnock, Lincolnshire** in 1283 by his half-brother, the 2nd Earl of Cornwall
- Styled as a gentleman, not nobility (because of illegitimacy)
- Killed at the **Siege of Berwick** in 1296 during the Scottish Wars

**His Significance:**
- Though illegitimate, Richard was acknowledged and granted land
- His descendants became the **Cornwall family of Burford**, Shropshire
- Sometimes styled "Barons of Burford" (though not formally peers)
- Later seated at Berington, Herefordshire

Historian Walter Lee Sheppard Jr. noted: *"It is interesting to note that through the family of Cornwall, uninterrupted male descent from the Plantagenets, with but this one illegitimacy, still exists."*

This means Richard of Cornwall represents the **only one break** (illegitimacy) in an otherwise legitimate line stretching back through the Plantagenets.

### The Cornwall Generations

From Richard of Cornwall (c.1252-1296) down to Matilda Cornwall (c.1388-1468), the Cornwall family remained in Shropshire as landed gentry:

1. **Richard of Cornwall** (c.1252-1296) → granted Manor of Thunnock
2. **Geoffrey Cornwall** (c.1288-c.1335) → inherited estates
3. **Richard Cornwall** (1311-1343) → continued line
4. **Geoffrey Cornwall** (c.1335-1365) → continued line
5. **Richard Cornwall** (1359-1443) → continued line
6. **Matilda Cornwall** (c.1388-1468) → married into Wolcott family

Over these generations (about 135 years from Richard to Matilda), the Cornwall family:
- Maintained their lands in Shropshire
- Remained part of the gentry class
- Intermarried with other gentry families
- Gradually became more "common" as distance from royalty increased

By the time Matilda married into the Wolcott family, the Cornwalls were still respectable gentry, but no longer closely connected to the royal court.

## Part 4: Richard, 1st Earl of Cornwall—A King's Son

**Richard of England, 1st Earl of Cornwall** (1209-1272) was one of the most powerful men in 13th-century Europe—and Matilda Cornwall's direct ancestor.

### Parentage and Royal Blood

**Father**: **King John of England** (1166-1216)
- The king made famous (or infamous) by the Robin Hood legends
- Forced to sign the Magna Carta in 1215
- Lost most of England's French territories

**Mother**: **Isabella of Angoulême** (c.1188-1246)
- Queen of England
- Richard was the **second legitimate son** of King John and Queen Isabella

**Siblings:**
- **Henry III** (1207-1272) - became King of England
- **Joan** (1210-1238) - married Alexander II of Scotland
- **Isabella** (1214-1241) - married Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II
- **Eleanor** (1215-1275) - married Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester

Richard was born into the very heart of medieval royal power.

### Richard's Extraordinary Life

Richard, 1st Earl of Cornwall, lived one of the most remarkable lives of the Middle Ages:

**Titles and Honors:**
- **Earl of Cornwall** (from 1225)
- **Count of Poitou** (1225-1243)
- **King of Germany** (elected 1257, though never crowned Holy Roman Emperor)
- One of the wealthiest men in Europe

**Military Career:**
- Fought in Wales and Scotland
- Participated in the Barons' War supporting his brother King Henry III
- Led a crusade to the Holy Land (1240-1241)

**Marriages:**
1. **Isabel Marshal** (1231-1240) - daughter of the Earl of Pembroke
2. **Sanchia of Provence** (1243-1261) - sister of the Queen of France
3. **Beatrice of Falkenburg** (1269-1272)

**Illegitimate Children:**
- **Richard of Cornwall** (c.1252-1296) - our ancestor - mother: Joan de Valletort
- Possibly others not recorded

**Wealth:**
- Controlled lucrative tin mines in Cornwall
- Received income from his many estates
- Used his wealth to become King of Germany (essentially buying votes)

**Death:**
- Died April 2, 1272 at Berkhamsted Castle
- Buried at Hailes Abbey, which he had founded

Richard was a king's son, a king's brother, and nearly Holy Roman Emperor. Through his illegitimate son, his blood flows to millions of modern descendants.

## Part 5: King John—The Magna Carta King

**King John of England** (c.1166-1216), Richard's father, is one of history's most controversial monarchs.

### Early Life

**Parentage:**
- **Father**: **Henry II of England** (1133-1189) - the first Plantagenet king
- **Mother**: **Eleanor of Aquitaine** (c.1122-1204) - one of the most powerful women of the Middle Ages

**Siblings:**
- **William** (1153-1156) - died young
- **Henry the Young King** (1155-1183) - crowned in father's lifetime but predeceased him
- **Matilda** (1156-1189) - married Henry the Lion, Duke of Saxony
- **Richard I "the Lionheart"** (1157-1199) - became king before John
- **Geoffrey** (1158-1186) - Duke of Brittany
- **Eleanor** (1161-1214) - Queen of Castile
- **Joan** (1165-1199) - Queen of Sicily

John was the **youngest** of eight children, nicknamed "Lackland" because there was little inheritance left for him.

### Becoming King

John became king in 1199 after his brother Richard I (the Lionheart) died. His reign was tumultuous:

**Major Events:**
- **Loss of Normandy** (1204) - lost most English possessions in France to Philip II
- **Conflict with the Pope** - excommunicated 1209-1213, England placed under interdict
- **Baronial rebellion** - nobles revolted against his arbitrary rule
- **Magna Carta** (1215) - forced to sign, limiting royal power
- **First Barons' War** (1215-1217) - civil war following Magna Carta
- **Death** (1216) - died during the civil war, possibly from dysentery

**Marriages:**
1. **Isabella of Gloucester** (1189-1199, annulled)
2. **Isabella of Angoulême** (1200-1216)

**Children with Isabella of Angoulême:**
- **Henry III** (1207-1272) - became king at age 9
- **Richard, 1st Earl of Cornwall** (1209-1272) - **our ancestor**
- **Joan** (1210-1238)
- **Isabella** (1214-1241)
- **Eleanor** (1215-1275)

### Historical Reputation

King John is remembered as:
- **Bad King John** in popular culture (Robin Hood villain)
- A failure in warfare (lost French territories)
- A tyrant (Magna Carta was a response to his abuses)
- BUT also: an effective administrator, patron of the navy, complex political figure

Whatever his failings as a king, John was the father of Richard of Cornwall, linking our family to the Plantagenet dynasty.

## Part 6: Henry II Plantagenet—The Founder of the Dynasty

At the top of our royal lineage stands **Henry II Plantagenet** (1133-1189), King John's father and one of the greatest monarchs in European history.

### The First Plantagenet King

**Henry II** (reigned 1154-1189) founded the **House of Plantagenet**, which would rule England for 331 years (1154-1485) through three branches:
- **Angevin Plantagenets** (1154-1399): Henry II through Richard II
- **House of Lancaster** (1399-1461, 1470-1471): Henry IV through Henry VI
- **House of York** (1461-1470, 1471-1485): Edward IV through Richard III

### Henry's Parentage and Inheritance

**Father**: **Geoffrey Plantagenet, Count of Anjou** (1113-1151)
- Count of Anjou, Maine, and Touraine
- Known as "Geoffrey the Handsome"
- Wore a sprig of **broom plant (planta genista)** in his hat - hence "Plantagenet"

**Mother**: **Empress Matilda** (1102-1167)
- Daughter of **Henry I of England**
- Holy Roman Empress (widowed)
- Fought for the English throne against Stephen of Blois

Henry inherited an extraordinary collection of territories:
- **Anjou, Maine, Touraine** - from his father
- **Normandy** - from his mother
- **England** - claimed through his mother (disputed, won by war)
- **Aquitaine** - through marriage to Eleanor

### Eleanor of Aquitaine

In 1152, Henry married **Eleanor of Aquitaine** (c.1122-1204), one of the most remarkable women in history.

**Eleanor's Background:**
- Duchess of Aquitaine (in her own right)
- Former Queen of France (married to Louis VII, annulled 1152)
- Brought the vast Duchy of Aquitaine to Henry

Together, Henry and Eleanor controlled an "**Angevin Empire**" stretching from Scotland to the Pyrenees—much larger than the French king's domain.

### Children of Henry and Eleanor

Henry and Eleanor had eight children together:

1. **William IX, Count of Poitiers** (1153-1156) - died young
2. **Henry the Young King** (1155-1183) - crowned as heir but rebelled against his father; died before inheriting
3. **Matilda, Duchess of Saxony** (1156-1189)
4. **Richard I "the Lionheart"** (1157-1199) - became king 1189; famous crusader
5. **Geoffrey II, Duke of Brittany** (1158-1186)
6. **Eleanor, Queen of Castile** (1161-1214)
7. **Joan, Queen of Sicily** (1165-1199)
8. **John "Lackland"** (c.1166-1216) - **our ancestor** - became king 1199

Plus illegitimate children, including:
- **Geoffrey, Archbishop of York** (c.1152-1212)
- **William Longespée, 3rd Earl of Salisbury** (c.1176-1226)

### Henry's Reign: Building an Empire

Henry II's reign (1154-1189) transformed England and established Plantagenet power:

**Legal Reforms:**
- Established **common law** system still used today
- Created system of royal justice with traveling judges
- Trial by jury began to develop
- Legal procedures that became foundation of English law

**Administrative Reforms:**
- Strengthened royal authority over feudal lords
- Created efficient bureaucracy
- Regular taxation system (Danegeld)
- Pipe Rolls (financial records) documented royal income

**Territorial Expansion:**
- Controlled England, Normandy, Anjou, Aquitaine
- Conquered Ireland (1171)
- Overlordship of Wales and Scotland

**Conflict with the Church:**
- Appointed Thomas Becket as Archbishop of Canterbury
- Quarreled with Becket over church courts
- Becket murdered in Canterbury Cathedral (1170)
- Henry did public penance but conflict continued

**Family Conflicts:**
- His sons (Henry, Richard, Geoffrey, John) rebelled against him multiple times
- Eleanor supported the rebellions; Henry imprisoned her for 16 years (1173-1189)
- Constant warfare within the family
- Richard and John allied with French King Philip II against their father

### Henry's Death and Legacy

Henry II died July 6, 1189 at Chinon, France:
- Defeated by his sons Richard and John (allied with Philip II of France)
- Forced to agree to humiliating peace terms
- Learned John (his favorite) had also betrayed him
- Died broken-hearted, saying "Shame, shame on a conquered king"
- Buried at Fontevraud Abbey, which became the Plantagenet mausoleum

**Legacy:**
- Founded a dynasty that ruled England for over 300 years
- Established legal and administrative systems that shaped English government
- Created an empire stretching across western Europe
- Father of two famous king sons: Richard the Lionheart and King John

Through King John, Henry II's blood flows to millions of descendants today—including our family.

## Part 7: The Significance of Royal Descent

### How Many People Descend from Henry II?

Genealogists estimate that **millions of people** with European ancestry descend from medieval royalty, including Henry II Plantagenet.

**Why so many?**
1. **Exponential growth**: Each generation doubles the number of ancestors (2 parents, 4 grandparents, 8 great-grandparents, etc.)
2. **Many children**: Medieval nobles often had many children, both legitimate and illegitimate
3. **Gateway ancestors**: People like Matilda Cornwall, through whom royal blood entered the general population
4. **Pedigree collapse**: Distant cousins marrying means we have fewer unique ancestors than the mathematical maximum

**The mathematics**:
- 29 generations from Henry II to present (assuming 25-30 years per generation)
- If each generation doubled with no overlap, that would be 2^29 = 536 million theoretical ancestors
- But with pedigree collapse (cousins marrying, etc.), the number of actual unique ancestors is much smaller
- Still, Henry II probably has millions of living descendants

### What Does Royal Descent Mean?

Having royal ancestors is genealogically interesting, but it's important to understand what it does and doesn't mean:

**What it DOES mean:**
- ✓ Our family history includes some of the most significant historical figures of medieval Europe
- ✓ We can trace our lineage back with reasonable certainty for 29 generations (850+ years)
- ✓ Our ancestors experienced the full range of human conditions: from kings to farmers
- ✓ We're part of the complex web of European genealogy

**What it DOESN'T mean:**
- ✗ We don't have any special rights, titles, or claims to nobility
- ✗ We're not unique (millions of people share this ancestry)
- ✗ It doesn't make us "better" than anyone else
- ✗ The royal blood is diluted over 29 generations to insignificance

As historian Walter Lee Sheppard Jr. noted about the Cornwall line: *"uninterrupted male descent from the Plantagenets, with but this one illegitimacy, still exists."* But this is a genealogical curiosity, not a meaningful claim to special status.

### Gateway Ancestors: Royalty Becomes Common

The phenomenon of "gateway ancestors" like Matilda Cornwall demonstrates how:
- Medieval social classes were more permeable than often assumed
- Illegitimate children of nobility sometimes founded gentry families
- Gentry families sometimes intermarried with wealthy commoners
- Over generations, "royal blood" dispersed into the general population

By the time John Wolcott immigrated to America in 1634, his Cornwall ancestry was probably unknown to him. He was a "woolen draper" (cloth merchant) from a market town—a commoner building a new life in Massachusetts.

Yet genealogically, he carried Plantagenet ancestry.

## Part 8: The DNA Evidence and Historical Proof

### Proving the Wolcott Line

The connection from our family to Henry II Plantagenet depends on proving each link in the chain. The most uncertain link historically was:

**John Wolcott I (1599-1638) → John Wolcott II (c.1634-1690)**

As discussed in the Abner Wolcott chapter, this connection lacked direct documentary evidence for centuries. In the early 2000s, the **Wolcott DNA Project** used Y-DNA testing to confirm:
- John Wolcott II was indeed the son of John Wolcott I
- The haplogroup Y22158 proves descent from the Watertown Wolcott line
- This confirms the lineage back through the English Wolcotts to Matilda Cornwall

**What this means:**
- ✓ The Wolcott portion of the lineage (steps 9-22) is confirmed by DNA
- ✓ Documentary evidence supports the Cornwall line (steps 22-27)
- ✓ The Plantagenet royal line (steps 27-30) is well-documented in historical records

### The Quality of Royal Genealogical Evidence

Medieval royal genealogy is generally well-documented because:
- Monarchs kept careful records of succession
- Chronicles recorded births, marriages, deaths of royalty
- Property transfers and legal documents mentioned royal family
- Multiple independent sources confirm royal lineages

For our lineage:
- **Henry II → John → Richard of Cornwall**: Excellent documentation (royal records, chronicles)
- **Richard of Cornwall → Richard of Cornwall (illegitimate)**: Good documentation (acknowledged illegitimate son, granted lands)
- **Richard of Cornwall → Cornwall family → Matilda Cornwall**: Fair documentation (family genealogies, property records)
- **Matilda Cornwall → Wolcott family → John Wolcott I**: Moderate documentation (English parish records, wills, property transfers)
- **John Wolcott I → modern descendants**: Good to excellent documentation (American colonial records, DNA evidence)

Overall, this lineage is well-supported by historical and scientific evidence.

## Part 11: What This Means for Our Identity

### We Are Products of History

This royal lineage reminds us that we are products of vast historical forces:
- The Norman Conquest (Henry II descended from William the Conqueror)
- The Plantagenet dynasty's rise and fall
- The Hundred Years' War between England and France
- The Protestant Reformation (affecting English/French relations)
- The Great Migration to New England
- The American Revolution (Abner's Loyalism)
- French-English tensions in Quebec
- The integration of English Loyalists into French Catholic Quebec

Our family participated in all of these historical currents.

### We Carry Multiple Identities

Through our ancestry, we can claim:
- **English heritage** (Plantagenet, Cornwall, Wolcott families)
- **French heritage** (through other lines like Couvent/Joyeuse)
- **American heritage** (colonial New England, United Empire Loyalist)
- **Canadian/Québécois heritage** (Loyalist settlement, French-Canadian integration)
- **Royal heritage** (Plantagenet descent through Matilda Cornwall)
- **Commoner heritage** (farmers, merchants, craftsmen for most of family history)

None of these identities is more "true" than the others. We are all of them.

### The Lesson of the Long View

Looking at 29 generations of ancestry teaches us:
- **Humility**: Royal ancestors don't make us special—millions share this heritage
- **Pride**: Our ancestors survived wars, plagues, famines, revolutions, and migrations
- **Perspective**: The distinctions between noble and common, English and French, American and Canadian fade over centuries
- **Connection**: We're part of an immense human story stretching back to the Middle Ages and forward to unknown futures

## Part 12: Conclusion—From Throne to Farm to Future

Our journey from **Henry II Plantagenet** (1133-1189), the most powerful king in 12th-century Europe, to a modern family is not unique. Millions of people share similar stories of descent from medieval royalty through gateway ancestors and immigrant lineages.

What makes our story special is that we can trace it. We know the names. We can map the path:
- From **Chinon Castle** where Henry II died
- To **Burford, Shropshire** where the Cornwall family settled
- To **Axbridge, Somerset** where John Wolcott was baptized
- To **Watertown, Massachusetts** where John settled in 1637
- To **New Haven, Vermont** where Abner farmed before the Revolution
- To **Sorel, Quebec** where Abner lived as a Loyalist refugee
- To **Sorel, Quebec** where Abner settled in the St. Lawrence River region
- To modern Quebec and beyond

The Plantagenet kings probably never imagined their descendants would farm in Quebec along the St. Lawrence River, speaking French, practicing Catholicism, and building new lives as British subjects in Canada.

But that's exactly what happened.

History is not just about kings and battles. It's about the choices ordinary people make across generations—choices that ripple forward in time, connecting medieval monarchs to modern families.

We are the inheritors of those choices, carriers of that history, and living proof that the past is never really past.

From kings to commoners, from England to France to America to Canada, from Plantagenet to Wolcott to Dansereau to Tanguay to Petit to Lapierre to us—the story continues.
