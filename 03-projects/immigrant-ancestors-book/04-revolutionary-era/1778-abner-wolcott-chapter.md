# The Loyalist: Abner Wolcott's Flight to Canada (1778)

## Part 1: A Choice Between Two Flags

In the spring of 1775, Abner Wolcott stood on his newly purchased land in New Haven, Vermont, and surveyed his future. He was twenty-eight years old, married to Catherine Griffen for three years, and the father of two young sons—John, age three, and Louis Abner, just one year old. The 150 acres he'd bought that year represented everything a man could hope for in colonial America: independence, opportunity, and the promise of prosperity for his growing family.

But the future Abner envisioned was about to be torn apart. That same spring, gunfire at Lexington and Concord shattered the peace of the colonies. By the time Abner's third son, Solomon, was born in 1778, Abner would be a prisoner, his land confiscated, his family separated, and his entire life upended.

The crime? Loyalty to the British Crown.

## Part 2: The Wolcott Legacy in America

Abner Wolcott came from a family with deep roots in New England. His ancestor, **John Wolcott**, had left the market town of Axbridge in Somerset, England, baptized there on May 13, 1599, the son of Edward Wolcott, a mercer and mayor of Axbridge. In 1634, John boarded the ship _Recovery_ at Weymouth, Dorset, sailing for the Massachusetts Bay Colony with the wave of Puritan migration that was transforming New England.

John Wolcott first settled in Salem, then moved to Cambridge in 1636, and finally to Watertown in 1637. He married three times—first to Mary Wrentmore in England, possibly to an unnamed woman, and finally to Winifred Crawford, a widow, in Watertown. By the time John died in 1638, he had established the Wolcott line in America.

For over a century, the Wolcott family spread throughout New England. By Abner's birth around 1747, Wolcotts were established farmers, craftsmen, and respectable members of colonial society. They had been in America for five generations—long enough to consider themselves Americans.

But when revolution came, Abner made a choice that would separate him from many of his fellow colonists: he chose the King.

## Part 3: Divided Loyalties in a Divided Land

Why did Abner Wolcott choose loyalty to Britain when so many of his neighbors chose independence?

We can only speculate. Many factors drove Loyalist sympathies in the American Revolution:
- **Economic ties** to British trade networks
- **Religious beliefs** that emphasized obedience to established authority
- **Fear of mob rule** and radical democracy
- **Genuine affection** for British institutions and the Crown
- **Practical calculation** that Britain would win the war

Vermont, where Abner had settled in 1775, was particularly divided. The region was contested between New York and New Hampshire, creating a complex political situation. Some Vermonters supported independence, others supported New York's claims, and still others supported the Crown.

Abner chose the Crown. And he acted on that choice.

## Part 4: Hauling Provisions for the King's Army

During the Revolutionary War, Abner worked as a **teamster**, hauling provisions and supplies for the British army in the northern campaigns. This was critical work—armies marched on their stomachs, and the logistics of keeping thousands of soldiers fed, clothed, and armed required constant supply trains.

But it was also dangerous work. To the American Patriots, teamsters hauling supplies for the British were traitors engaging in active support of the enemy. Supply routes were targeted. Teamsters could be captured or killed.

For years, Abner did this work, supporting the British cause even as the tide of war turned against them.

## Part 5: Saratoga and Capture (1777)

**September-October 1777**: The British northern campaign reached its climax at the **Battle of Saratoga**, one of the turning points of the Revolutionary War. British General John Burgoyne led his forces south from Canada, attempting to split New England from the other colonies by controlling the Hudson River Valley.

The campaign was a disaster. American forces under General Horatio Gates and Colonel Benedict Arnold (before his defection) surrounded Burgoyne's army. On October 17, 1777, Burgoyne surrendered—a humiliating British defeat that convinced France to enter the war on the American side.

Abner Wolcott was "with the British" during this campaign. Whether as a teamster in Burgoyne's supply train, as a scout, or in some other capacity, we don't know. But we know what happened:

**In 1777, Abner was taken prisoner by American forces.**

Imagine the moment: the British and Loyalist forces surrendering, American soldiers rounding up prisoners, Abner's anxiety about what would happen next. He knew the Patriots' hatred for Loyalists—those they called traitors to the American cause. His property in Vermont would be confiscated. His family was at risk. His future looked dark.

But Abner Wolcott was not ready to give up.

## Part 6: Escape to Canada (1778)

**In 1778, Abner escaped from captivity and fled north to Canada.**

The details of his escape are lost to history. How did he get away? Did he overpower a guard? Slip away in darkness? Bribe someone? We don't know. But we know he made it.

The journey north to British-controlled Canada would have been harrowing:
- **Winter travel** through wilderness (most escapes happened in colder months when rivers were frozen)
- **Enemy territory** all around—one wrong encounter and he'd be recaptured
- **No supplies** or support—he had to live off the land or rely on other Loyalist sympathizers
- **Leaving family behind**—Catherine and the boys would have to follow later, if at all

But Abner made it to safety.

## Part 7: The Refugee Camp at Sorel

Abner arrived at the **British military post and refugee camp at Sorel, Quebec**, where the Richelieu River meets the St. Lawrence. This strategic location had become a major gathering point for Loyalist refugees fleeing the American colonies.

What Abner found there was both hope and despair:

**Hope:**
- Safety from American forces
- British military protection
- Reunion with other Loyalists who had also lost everything
- The promise of eventual British victory

**Despair:**
- **Makeshift housing**—refugee camps were crowded, cold, and unsanitary
- **Poverty**—Loyalists had fled with little or nothing
- **Uncertainty**—Would they ever go home? Would Britain win? What would happen to their families?
- **Separation**—Many refugees were separated from loved ones

But eventually, Catherine joined him. She made the dangerous journey north with their three young sons—John (now six), Louis Abner (four), and baby Solomon. The Wolcott family was reunited in exile.

## Part 8: The Price of Loyalty

By 1783, when the **Treaty of Paris** ended the Revolutionary War and recognized American independence, the price of Abner Wolcott's loyalty was clear:

**What He Lost:**
- **150 acres** of land in New Haven, Vermont (purchased just eight years earlier)
- All **property and possessions** left behind when fleeing
- His **established life** in New England
- **Extended family** who may have remained in the new United States
- **Economic security** built over years of work
- The **country of his birth**—he was now an exile

The British had lost. The Loyalists had bet on the wrong side. Abner could never safely return to Vermont.

But the British government, to its credit, tried to compensate those who had remained loyal.

## Part 9: British Compensation and New Beginnings (1783)

In **1783**, Abner Wolcott received **compensation from the British government** for his losses in Vermont. This was part of a massive compensation program for Loyalists who had remained faithful to the Crown and lost property as a result.

The amount Abner received is not recorded, but for thousands of Loyalists, these payments allowed them to start over. Governor **Frederick Haldimand** had purchased the seigneurie of Sorel for the Crown during the war for strategic military reasons, and now he was establishing a town there to accommodate Loyalist refugees permanently.

Abner used his compensation to settle in the Sorel region. Records indicate he held land on one of the islands in the St. Lawrence River near Sorel (survey records from the îles Bouchard mention his name), and his children were baptized in Catholic churches at St. Sulpice and Verchères, towns on opposite sides of the river. There, he would farm, raise his family, and build a new life in British territory.

## Part 10: A New Life in French Catholic Quebec

Life in the Sorel region placed the Wolcott family in a new cultural world:

**English Protestant Loyalists in French Catholic Quebec**

The Wolcotts were:
- English-speaking in a French-speaking region
- Protestant in an overwhelmingly Catholic society
- Newcomers in a society with deep roots going back to New France
- Farmers trying to adapt to Quebec's seigneurial system

The adjustment required compromises. The most significant involved religion.

**The Wolcott Children's Conversions**

Abner's children were baptized in **Catholic churches**—either at **St. Sulpice** or **Verchères**, towns on opposite shores of the St. Lawrence from their island home. This was likely a practical necessity—in Quebec, Catholic baptism was often required for legal recognition.

Then, in **1798**, four of Abner's children—**John, Louis Abner, Solomon, and Catherine**—formally **renounced their Protestant faith and joined the Catholic Church**.

Why?
- **Marriage**—In Quebec, marrying into French-Canadian families often required Catholic affiliation
- **Social integration**—Being Catholic smoothed interactions with neighbors
- **Economic opportunity**—Catholic affiliation could help with land transactions and business
- **Cultural adaptation**—The children were growing up in French Catholic Quebec

But Abner and Catherine never converted. When they died, they were **buried in the Protestant cemetery** at Christ Church Anglican in Sorel. The old Loyalists held to their faith, even as their children adapted to their new world.

## Part 11: A Brief Return to America (c. 1800)

Around **1800**, Abner and his son John made a journey back to **Vergennes, Vermont**—back to the United States, back to the land Abner had fled twenty-two years earlier.

Why did they go? We can only speculate:
- To check on property or attempt to reclaim land?
- To visit family or old friends?
- To explore whether they could resettle in the now-independent United States?
- To close a final chapter of Abner's American life?

Whatever the reason, they didn't stay. The Wolcotts were Canadians now.

## Part 12: The Oath of Allegiance (1803)

Soon after returning from Vermont, Abner and his family took the **Oath of Allegiance to the British Crown in 1803**. This formal act confirmed their status as British subjects in Lower Canada and likely secured their right to remain on their land.

It was also a statement: Abner Wolcott had made his choice in 1775, and he was standing by it. He had been loyal to the Crown during the Revolution, had suffered for that loyalty, and would remain a British subject for the rest of his life.

## Part 13: Final Years (1803-1833)

Abner lived the remainder of his long life quietly in the Sorel region:
- **Farming** and managing his land
- **Watching his children** marry and establish their own families
- **Witnessing the integration** of his family into French-Canadian society
- Living as a respected British subject and United Empire Loyalist

**Second Marriage (1821):**
At age **seventy-four**, Abner married **Dorothy Redman**, a widow. His first wife, Catherine, who had followed him into exile and shared his years of hardship, had presumably died. This late-life marriage likely provided companionship in his final years.

**Will (1825):**
At age **seventy-eight**, Abner drafted his will, dated 1825:
- He left his estate to his wife Dolly (Dorothy)
- After her death, the estate would be divided among his **ten children**
- This will later caused disputes among his heirs (a common occurrence in cases of second marriages with children from a first marriage)

**Death (August 1, 1833):**
Abner Wolcott died at **Sorel, Quebec** on **August 1, 1833**, at the age of **eighty-six**. He was buried at **Christ Church Anglican Cemetery, Sorel**—in the Protestant cemetery, as he had lived, faithful to his British Protestant identity.

He had lived to see:
- The American Revolution and the birth of the United States
- The exile of thousands of Loyalists to Canada
- The War of 1812 between Britain and the United States
- The gradual integration of his own family into French-Canadian Catholic society
- His children and grandchildren flourishing in their new homeland

Abner had chosen loyalty, paid the price, and built a new life. His legacy would continue through his descendants in Quebec.

## Part 14: The Loyalist Legacy

### United Empire Loyalist Honor

Abner Wolcott is recognized as a **United Empire Loyalist** (UEL), a designation of honor in Canada for those who remained loyal to the British Crown during the American Revolution and came to Canada because of that loyalty.

The UEL designation carries significance:
- Recognition of sacrifice and loyalty
- Historical acknowledgment of the Loyalist contribution to Canadian identity
- Heritage rights for descendants

Abner's story was shared by thousands:
- Approximately **40,000-50,000 Loyalists** fled to Canada during and after the Revolution
- About **6,000-7,000** settled in Quebec
- They faced hardship, loss, and the challenge of rebuilding from nothing
- Their loyalty helped ensure Canada remained British while the Thirteen Colonies became the United States

### Quebec Descendants

Abner's descendants became woven into the fabric of Quebec society:
- Many married into French-Canadian families
- Converted to Catholicism (while some maintained Protestant faith)
- Adopted French-Canadian customs and language
- Some kept the Wolcott surname; others used variations (Wilcott, Woolcott)

One notable descendant line:
- **Marie Wolcott** (1788-1867) married Pierre Dansereau
- Their daughter **Julie Dansereau** (1828-1894) married Joseph Tanguay
- Their daughter **Regina Tanguay** (1872-1942) married Télesphore Petit
- Their daughter **Marie-Alice Petit** (1897-1974) married Joseph Lapierre
- Their daughter **Thérèse Lapierre** (1923-2005) continued the line

Through this lineage, thousands of modern Quebecers and Americans can trace their ancestry back to Abner Wolcott, the Loyalist who chose the Crown and fled to Canada.

## Part 15: The Genealogical Mystery—and Its Solution

For genealogists tracing the Wolcott line, there was long a frustrating problem: **the connection between John Wolcott I (1599-1638) of Watertown, Massachusetts, and his supposed son John Wolcott II (c.1634-1690) could not be definitively proven through documentary evidence.**

### The Problem

**John Wolcott I:**
- Baptized May 13, 1599 in Axbridge, Somerset, England
- Son of Edward Wolcott (a mercer and mayor of Axbridge) and Marie Phippen
- Immigrated to Massachusetts in 1634 on the ship _Recovery_
- Died in Watertown, Massachusetts before June 5, 1638

**John Wolcott II:**
- Born about 1634 (deposed in 1664 "aged about thirty years")
- First appeared in Newbury, Massachusetts in the 1650s
- Married Mary Thurlow in Newbury, November 20, 1653
- Died at Brookfield, Massachusetts, September 21, 1690

**The Missing Link:**
- John I died in 1638 when John II would have been a child
- No baptismal record, no direct mention in wills, no contemporary documents explicitly stating "John II, son of John I"
- The connection was based on **circumstantial evidence**:
  - Timing (John II's age consistent with being John I's son)
  - Location (John II owned land at Watertown, where John I lived)
  - Family connections (John II sold land to Nathaniel Clark, whose father William married Sarah Woolcott, John I's daughter—making her possibly John II's half-sister)

Some genealogists accepted this circumstantial evidence; others questioned whether the connection was proven.

For all of Abner Wolcott's descendants trying to trace their line back to England, this uncertainty was frustrating. Could they claim John Wolcott I as their ancestor or not?

### The Solution: DNA Evidence

In the early 2000s, modern DNA technology provided the answer.

The **Wolcott DNA Project**, begun by Charles W. Wolcott and John B. Wolcott in 2004, used Y-DNA testing (which traces the male line) to resolve genealogical questions in the Wolcott family.

**The Discovery:**
- Descendants of **John Wolcott of Watertown** (John I) belong to a specific Y-DNA haplogroup called **Y22158**
- This haplogroup branched off from haplogroup Y20600 around **1570 AD**—right around the time of John I's ancestors in England
- **No other branches** of the Wolcott family belong to Y22158
- All tested descendants of **John Wolcott II** also carry **Y22158**

**The Definitive Proof:**
In particular, Gary Lynn Wolcott's positive test result for DNA SNP Y22158 provided **definitive proof** that descendants of later Wolcotts—including Silas Wolcott of Pennsylvania and the Wolcott community of Brookfield, Massachusetts—descend from **John Wolcott I of Watertown**.

This means: **John Wolcott II was indeed the son of John Wolcott I.**

The circumstantial evidence had been right all along. DNA confirmed what genealogists had believed based on timing, location, and family connections.

### What This Means for Abner's Descendants

For descendants of Abner Wolcott, this DNA evidence is significant:
- ✓ The Wolcott line is **confirmed** back to John Wolcott I (1599-1638)
- ✓ Through John I, the line goes back to **Edward Wolcott** (c.1570-1640)
- ✓ Through Edward, back to **John Wolcott** (c.1545-1589) of Tolland, Somerset
- ✓ And further back through the Wolcott family of England

And through the maternal line, through **Matilda Cornwall** (c.1388-1468) who married into the Wolcott family, the line reaches back to the **Plantagenet kings of England**.

## Part 16: Two Revolutions, Two Choices

It's worth noting the irony of Abner Wolcott's life in the context of broader family history:

**Abner's Choice (1775-1778):** Loyalty to the British Crown
- Fought against American independence
- Fled to Canada to remain under British rule
- Lived as a British subject for the rest of his life

**Clément Gosselin's Choice (1775-1783):** Support for American Independence
- Another ancestor from the same family tree (though not Abner's direct line)
- French-Canadian who fought *for* the Americans against the British
- Recruited for Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment, Continental Army
- Spied for George Washington
- Wounded at Yorktown

Two men, two parts of the same extended family, two opposite choices in the same revolution.

History remembers both. Clément Gosselin is honored as a Patriot Ancestor by the Daughters and Sons of the American Revolution. Abner Wolcott is honored as a United Empire Loyalist in Canada.

Both paid the price for their convictions. Both lost property, endured hardship, and rebuilt their lives. Both left legacies that continue through their descendants.

## Part 17: Reflections on Loyalty

What should we make of Abner Wolcott's choice?

In the United States, the Revolution is celebrated as the birth of freedom, and the Loyalists are often forgotten—or remembered as being "on the wrong side of history." But Abner's story reminds us that the Revolution was a civil war, dividing communities, families, and even individuals who had to make impossible choices.

Abner chose loyalty. We can respect that choice even if we're glad the Revolution succeeded. He believed in something, stood by his principles, suffered for them, and built a new life accordingly. That takes courage.

In Canada, the Loyalists are honored as founders. They helped ensure that British North America remained British while the Thirteen Colonies became independent. They contributed to Canadian identity as a nation that valued "peace, order, and good government" rather than "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."

Abner Wolcott was part of that founding. His choice to flee north rather than submit to the American rebels helped create Canada as we know it.

And for his descendants—Americans and Canadians alike—Abner represents something important: the complexity of history, the difficulty of moral choices, and the resilience of those who lose everything and rebuild.

## Part 18: Conclusion—The Long View

Abner Wolcott died in 1833, forty-nine years after the end of the Revolutionary War. He had lived long enough to see that the United States, the country he refused to join, was flourishing. He had also seen Canada develop as a British dominion, loyal to the Crown he had never abandoned.

Looking back from our present day, we can see the long arc of Abner's legacy:

From **John Wolcott** leaving England in 1634, to **Abner Wolcott** leaving America in 1778, the family crossed oceans and borders, adapting to new worlds while maintaining continuity across generations.

From Abner's island in the St. Lawrence River to descendants scattered across North America, the Wolcott name (and its variations) continues.

From Protestant English roots to French Catholic integration, the family shows the fluidity of identity and the power of adaptation.

And from the royal Plantagenet line through Matilda Cornwall to a Loyalist farmer on an island in Quebec, the family tree shows how history is made not just by kings and generals, but by ordinary people making difficult choices in difficult times.

Abner Wolcott chose loyalty. He paid the price. He started over. He endured.

His story deserves to be remembered.
