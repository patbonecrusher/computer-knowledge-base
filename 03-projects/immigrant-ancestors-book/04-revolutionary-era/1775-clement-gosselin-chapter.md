# Clément Gosselin - The Quebec Revolutionary (1775-1781)

> Research Notes: [[../../../../02-subjects/genealogy/families/clement-gosselin|Clément Gosselin Research]]

---

## Opening Hook

December 31, 1775. A blizzard rages over Quebec City. Through the swirling snow, approximately 200 French-Canadian men advance alongside American forces, attacking the fortified city in the dead of night.

Among them: twenty-eight-year-old Clément Gosselin, a carpenter from Île d'Orléans.

The attack will fail catastrophically. General Richard Montgomery will be killed. Benedict Arnold will be wounded. Many will be captured.

But Clément Gosselin's war has just begun.

Over the next six years, he will recruit hundreds of French-Canadians for the Continental Army. He will command a company in Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment. He will spy for George Washington, traveling secretly through Quebec gathering intelligence. He will lose everything—his property seized, his family persecuted. And he will be wounded at Yorktown, present for the climactic American victory.

This is the story of a French-Canadian revolutionary who bet everything on American independence—and paid a terrible price.

---

## A Carpenter from Île d'Orléans

### Born Into an Established Quebec Family

Clément Gosselin was born on **June 12, 1747**, in the parish of Sainte-Famille on **Île d'Orléans**, just east of Quebec City in the St. Lawrence River.

His family had deep roots in New France:
- The Gosselin family had emigrated from Normandy, France around 1652
- Nearly 100 years in Quebec by Clément's birth
- His father Gabriel was a carpenter, a respected trade
- Clément was the youngest of a large family

Île d'Orléans was one of the most prosperous agricultural regions of Quebec—picturesque, French-speaking, Catholic, close enough to Quebec City to be connected to the political world but rural enough to maintain traditional ways.

### Growing Up French-Canadian

Clément grew up in a world that was:
- **Thoroughly French**: Language, culture, customs unchanged from France
- **Devoutly Catholic**: The Church was the center of community life
- **Recently conquered**: In 1760, when Clément was 13, the British conquered New France

That conquest changed everything. Suddenly, French-Canadians lived under British rule—a Protestant, English-speaking power governing a Catholic, French-speaking people.

### Becoming a Carpenter

Like his father, Clément learned the carpenter's trade—skilled work requiring:
- Years of training
- Mathematical knowledge for measurements and calculations
- Physical strength and endurance
- Artistic sensibility for fine work

By his twenties, he was an established carpenter living in **La Pocatière**, about 75 miles northeast of Quebec City on the south shore of the St. Lawrence.

### Life Under British Rule

For fifteen years (1760-1775), French-Canadians adapted to British governance. The British, to their credit:
- Allowed French language and civil law to continue
- Permitted Catholic worship (unusual for a Protestant power)
- Appointed some French-Canadians to administrative positions

But tensions remained:
- Economic power concentrated among British merchants
- French-Canadians excluded from many government positions
- Cultural resentment simmered
- Many hoped for French reconquest or some other change

When the American Revolution erupted in 1775, it presented French-Canadians with a choice: support the British who ruled them, or support the American rebels who promised liberty?

Clément Gosselin chose rebellion.

## The American Invasion of Quebec

### Fall 1775: Two Armies Approach

In the fall of 1775, the Continental Congress authorized an invasion of Quebec, hoping to bring it into the rebellion as the "14th colony."

Two American armies marched north:

**Richard Montgomery**: Moved up Lake Champlain, captured Montreal in November 1775

**Benedict Arnold**: Led a grueling expedition through the Maine wilderness—one of the most difficult military marches in American history

The plan: Join forces at Quebec City and capture the capital of British Canada.

### French-Canadian Sympathies

Many French-Canadians were sympathetic to the Americans:
- Resentment of British rule
- Hope for French support (France hadn't yet entered the war)
- American promises of liberty and self-governance
- Economic grievances against British merchants

But others opposed the invasion:
- The Catholic Church generally supported British authority
- Fear of Protestant American domination
- Loyalty oaths to the British crown
- Pragmatic recognition that Britain was powerful

French-Canadian society was divided. Approximately 200 chose to actively join the American attack.

Clément was one of them.

### December 31, 1775: The Battle of Quebec

On the night of **December 30-31, 1775**, American forces attacked Quebec City during a fierce snowstorm.

The plan was audacious:
- Montgomery's force would attack from the west
- Arnold's from the east
- They would meet inside the Lower Town and storm the Upper Town
- French-Canadian volunteers would support both columns

#### The Attack

What actually happened was disaster:

**Montgomery's column**: Almost immediately, General Montgomery was killed by grapeshot from a cannon. His men panicked and retreated.

**Arnold's column**: Benedict Arnold was shot in the leg early in the attack. His men pushed into the Lower Town but were trapped and forced to surrender. Daniel Morgan and hundreds of others were captured.

**Casualties**:
- Americans: 60 killed, 400+ captured
- British/Canadian defenders: Minimal losses

The attack was a complete failure.

#### Clément's Role

Clément was among the approximately 200 French-Canadians who participated. We don't know exactly which column he was in or what specific actions he took. But we know:

- He survived the battle (unlike Montgomery)
- He wasn't captured (unlike hundreds of others)
- He escaped Quebec City somehow

And crucially: **the defeat didn't shake his commitment**. While some French-Canadians who had supported the Americans melted away after the failure, Clément doubled down.

## Recruiter for the Revolution

### A Dangerous Mission (January - May 1776)

After the failed assault, the remnants of the American force besieged Quebec City through the brutal winter. They desperately needed reinforcements.

Clément undertook a perilous assignment: **recruiting French-Canadians to join the Continental Army**.

From **January to May 1776**, he traveled throughout parishes on the south shore of the St. Lawrence:
- From Pointe-Lévy (across from Quebec City)
- To Sainte-Anne-de-la-Pocatière
- Visiting French-Canadian communities
- Convincing men to join a cause that had just suffered catastrophic defeat

This required extraordinary courage:
- British authorities considered this **treason**
- Anyone caught recruiting faced imprisonment, property seizure, or worse
- His family would suffer for his actions
- He was asking men to abandon their families and farms
- For a cause that had just failed spectacularly

But Clément believed. And he had a gift for persuasion.

### The Battle of Saint-Pierre (March 25, 1776)

On **March 25, 1776**, the division among French-Canadians became violent at the **Battle of Saint-Pierre**.

**Pro-American forces**:
- 150 French-Canadians (recruited by Clément and others)
- 80 American soldiers

**Pro-British forces**:
- 150 French-Canadians recruited by Liénard de Beaujeu (fighting for the British)

French-Canadians fought French-Canadians. Neighbor against neighbor. Family against family.

The pro-American side won—a small victory in a larger failing campaign.

Clément was there, fighting not just against the British, but against other French-Canadians who had made different choices.

## Captain in the 2nd Canadian Regiment

### March 1776: Official Commission

In **March 1776**, Clément officially joined **Moses Hazen's 2nd Canadian Regiment** as **Captain of the 7th Company**.

This made him an officer in the Continental Army—a formal commission from the Continental Congress, equivalent in rank and pay to any other captain in the American forces.

### The 2nd Canadian Regiment

The **2nd Canadian Regiment** (also called "Congress's Own" or "Hazen's Regiment") was unique:

**Composition**:
- Primarily French-speaking Catholics from Quebec
- Some English-speaking Canadians
- A few American volunteers
- Unlike any other unit in the Continental Army

**Commander**: Colonel Moses Hazen
- A controversial figure—some called him a hero, others a scoundrel
- Had lived in Quebec before the Revolution
- Deeply committed to bringing Quebec into the American union
- Would clash repeatedly with other Continental officers

**Situation**:
- Recruited from Quebec but could never return there under British rule
- Fighting for American independence but their own homeland remained British
- Many would eventually settle in the United States after the war
- A regiment without a home

### Recruiting Success

As a captain, Clément continued recruiting. His success was remarkable:

Along with Pierre Ayotte (another recruiter), Clément raised approximately **150 men** who joined the Americans.

In context:
- This was after the failed Quebec invasion
- Under British occupation
- With the Catholic Church opposing the rebellion
- Risking everything to join

That 150 men still chose to fight speaks to Clément's persuasiveness and the genuine sympathy many French-Canadians felt for the American cause.

### The American Retreat from Canada (May 1776)

By May 1776, the American invasion of Canada had completely collapsed:
- Reinforcements couldn't save the situation
- Smallpox devastated American forces
- British reinforcements arrived
- The Americans retreated south

The 2nd Canadian Regiment retreated with them. For men like Clément, this meant:
- Abandoning Quebec
- Leaving families behind
- Knowing their properties would be seized
- Becoming permanent exiles

But they marched south with the Continental Army, committed to the cause even as their hopes of liberating Quebec died.

## George Washington's Spy

### The Intelligence Mission (1778-1780)

From **summer 1778 until summer 1780**, Clément undertook perhaps his most dangerous work: **returning secretly to British-controlled Quebec as a spy**.

Think about this: He was a known rebel whose properties had been seized. British authorities would arrest him on sight. Yet he went back, repeatedly, to gather intelligence for George Washington.

His missions:
- **Assess British military strength** in Quebec
- **Gauge sentiment** among French-Canadians
- **Spread rumors** of an imminent American invasion (psychological warfare)
- **Identify potential supporters** for another invasion attempt
- **Report everything to Washington**

This made him **George Washington's personal Canadian military intelligence informant**—a remarkable level of trust.

### The Spy Network

Clément didn't work alone. He organized a **network of informants** throughout Quebec:
- People who could observe British movements
- Sympathizers who could assess local sentiment
- Contacts who could pass messages
- A functioning intelligence apparatus behind enemy lines

Managing this required:
- Tradecraft (codes, secret messages, safe houses)
- Courage (constant risk of capture)
- Judgment (whom to trust)
- Commitment (why do this dangerous work?)

### The November 1778 Letter to Washington

On **November 23, 1778**, Clément wrote directly to General George Washington. The letter detailed:

**His intelligence work**: What he had learned about British strength and French-Canadian sentiment

**Plans for another invasion**: Clément reported on preparations for Congress to launch another attack on Quebec (which never materialized)

**A desperate plea**: "All our possessions were seized and that our families were at the mercy of the world."

This last part is heartbreaking. Clément's family in Quebec was suffering terribly:
- Properties confiscated by British authorities
- No income or support
- Subject to harassment and persecution
- "At the mercy of the world"

He was begging Washington for help for his family—the price he and they paid for his commitment to the Revolution.

### The October 1778 Intelligence Report

In October 1778, Clément and his spy network produced a comprehensive **report on the state of Quebec** for Congress.

This report:
- Assessed British military capabilities
- Analyzed political sentiment among French-Canadians
- Evaluated the feasibility of another invasion
- Demonstrated Clément's sophisticated understanding of intelligence gathering

Congress was seriously considering another invasion attempt in 1779-1780. Clément's intelligence was crucial to those deliberations (though ultimately the invasion never happened—France's entry into the war changed strategic calculations).

## Yorktown: The Climactic Battle

### October 1781: The Siege

By October 1781, the Revolutionary War was nearing its climax. A combined American and French army had trapped British General Cornwallis's army at Yorktown, Virginia.

The siege was the largest military operation of the war:
- 17,000 American and French troops
- British army of 8,000 trapped
- Massive artillery bombardment
- Systematic trench warfare

The 2nd Canadian Regiment was there. Captain Clément Gosselin was there.

After six years of war—the disaster at Quebec, the recruiting, the spying, the exile, the sacrifice—Clément was present for the battle that would win American independence.

### October 4, 1781: Wounded

On **October 4, 1781**, during the siege operations, Clément was **severely wounded**.

A British cannonball struck near his position. The impact didn't kill him directly, but sent **wood splinters flying**. Shrapnel tore into his leg, causing serious injury.

Artillery wounds were notoriously difficult to treat:
- Infection was common (no antibiotics)
- Amputation was often necessary
- Many died not from the initial wound but from complications
- Recovery was long and painful

Clément survived, but the injury likely affected him for the rest of his life.

### October 19, 1781: Victory

Despite his wound, Clément was still there on **October 19, 1781**, when **Lord Cornwallis surrendered**.

The British army marched out and laid down their arms. The war was effectively over (though peace wouldn't be formally signed until 1783).

American independence was won.

For Clément, wounded and watching the British surrender, the victory must have been bittersweet:
- **America was free**—the cause he'd fought for succeeded
- **Quebec remained British**—his own homeland would not be liberated
- **His family had suffered terribly**—properties seized, persecution endured
- **He could never go home**—returning to Quebec meant arrest

But America was free. And he had been part of making that happen.

## The Price of Revolution

### What Clément Lost

Let's catalog what Clément Gosselin sacrificed for the American Revolution:

**Property**:
- All possessions in Quebec seized by British authorities
- Land confiscated
- Economic ruin

**Family**:
- His relatives in Quebec persecuted
- "At the mercy of the world" according to his letter
- Unable to protect or support them

**Home**:
- Could never safely return to Quebec under British rule
- Exiled from Île d'Orléans, from La Pocatière
- Cut off from the landscape and community of his birth

**Security**:
- Six years of war
- Constant danger as a spy
- Wounded at Yorktown
- No guarantee of pension or support after the war

**For what?**:
- American independence (achieved)
- But not Quebec's liberation (failed)

### His Three Marriages and Large Family

Clément married three times and fathered at least 25 children—a remarkable family despite the chaos of war and exile:

**First Marriage: Marie Beuve Dionne (1770)**
- Married **January 22, 1770** in Sainte-Anne-de-la-Pocatière, Province de Québec
- She was born March 5, 1755
- This was just months before he joined the American invasion
- Children from this marriage (born 1770-1778):
  - Clément (born November 21, 1770 in Kamouraska)
  - Catherine (born July 22, 1773 in Ste-Anne-de-la-Pocatière)
  - Marie Geneviève (born May 5, 1775)
  - Marguerite (born October 13, 1777)
  - Marie Elisabeth (born 1778)

These were the children "at the mercy of the world" when he wrote to Washington in 1778. Marie Beuve was left alone with five small children while her husband fought for the Americans. We don't know when or how this marriage ended—she may have died, or the separation of war may have dissolved the marriage.

**Second Marriage: Marie Charlotte Ouimet (1787)**
- Married **January 15, 1787** in Longueuil, Province de Québec
- She was born February 10, 1761
- This was after the war, when he had returned to Quebec
- Children from this marriage (born 1788-1791):
  - Charlotte (born June 15, 1788 in Longueuil)
  - Toussaint (born October 26, 1789 in Longueuil)
  - Clément (born May 15, 1791 in Saint-Hyacinthe)

**Third Marriage: Marie Catherine Monty (1791)**
- Married **May 12, 1791** in Saint-Hyacinthe, Bas-Canada
- She was born July 30, 1769
- This marriage produced the most children (born 1792-1811):
  - Multiple children including several who died in infancy (listed as "Anonyme")
  - Catherine Appolline (born September 17, 1794)
  - Catherine Apolline (born June 26, 1795)
  - Pierre (born April 5, 1797)
  - Ferdinand (born July 31, 1798)
  - Pierre Jacques (born July 29, 1799)
  - Jean Baptiste (born July 22, 1802)
  - Marguerite Apolline (born October 10, 1803)
  - Marie Geneviève (born September 19, 1804)
  - Jean Baptiste (born October 8, 1806)
  - Marie (born June 20, 1810)
  - Michel (born October 14, 1811)

The size of his family is remarkable: he fathered children from age 23 to age 64, across nearly 40 years and through the entirety of the Revolutionary War.

### After the War: A Life Rebuilt

**Promotion and Discharge (June 1783)**

In **June 1783**, after the Treaty of Versailles formally ended the war, Clément was promoted to **Major** and honorably discharged from the Continental Army.

This promotion was crucial—it recognized six years of extraordinary service. But there was a problem: **the promotion was not properly recorded in the regimental records**. This bureaucratic error would haunt his family for decades.

**1,000 Acres in New York (1784)**

In **1784**, New York State granted **1,000 acres of land on the shores of Lake Champlain** to Canadian veterans of the Continental Army. This was their reward for service—land to build new lives after being exiled from Quebec.

Clément received his 1,000 acres. But he soon **sold the land**, probably preferring to return to his carpenter's trade rather than becoming a frontier farmer.

**Life at Champlain, New York (1783-1791)**

From 1783 to early 1791, Clément lived at **Champlain, New York**—a small town right on the Quebec border, just south of Montreal.

This location is revealing: He couldn't safely return to Quebec permanently (British authorities would arrest him), but he lived as close as possible. Perhaps to be near family still in Quebec? Perhaps hoping conditions would change?

During these years, he:
- Worked as a carpenter
- Married his second wife, Marie Charlotte Ouimet (1787)
- Had three children with her
- Lived in American territory but in a French-speaking community

**Return to Quebec (1791-1815)**

In **early 1791**, Clément made a remarkable decision: he **returned to Quebec**.

The war had been over for eight years. Perhaps British authorities were no longer actively pursuing him. Perhaps he received some guarantee of safety. Perhaps he simply missed home too much to stay away.

His movements in Quebec:
- **1791-1800**: Lived in the **Saint-Hyacinthe region** in Lower Canada
  - Married his third wife, Marie Catherine Monty (May 1791)
  - Had many children with her
  - Resumed his carpentry work
  - Rebuilt his life in his homeland

- **~1800**: Briefly stayed at **Sainte-Marguerite-de-Blairfindie (L'Acadie)**

- **1803-1815**: Settled at **Saint-Luc**, where he lived for twelve years
  - Continued having children (his last child born 1811, when he was 64)
  - Worked as a carpenter
  - Lived quietly as an old revolutionary

**Final Move to Beekmantown (1815)**

In **early 1815**, at age 67, Clément left Quebec one last time and moved to **Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York**—back to American territory.

Why? We don't know for certain. Perhaps:
- To be closer to other Continental Army veterans
- To ensure his widow would receive his military pension
- Because he was dying and wanted to die in the country he'd fought for
- To escape ongoing tensions in Quebec

**Death (March 9, 1816)**

Clément Gosselin died on **March 9, 1816** at age 68 in **Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York**.

He was buried in **East Beekmantown Cemetery**, where his grave remained unmarked for over 200 years.

He died having outlived the Revolution by 33 years. By 1816:
- The War of 1812 had just ended (more US-British conflict)
- Canada remained British (would until Confederation in 1867)
- Few Americans remembered the 2nd Canadian Regiment
- His extraordinary service was largely forgotten

## Legacy and Recognition

### Forgotten for Generations

For most of American and Canadian history, Clément Gosselin was forgotten.

**In the United States**:
- The Revolutionary War narrative focused on the 13 colonies
- The failed Quebec invasion was an embarrassment, quickly glossed over
- The 2nd Canadian Regiment was a minor footnote
- French-Canadian patriots were barely mentioned

**In Quebec/Canada**:
- The British victory was the official narrative
- Those who supported the Americans were viewed as traitors
- The Catholic Church had opposed the Revolution
- Clément's story didn't fit the national mythology

### The Pension Injustice and Congressional Redress (1860)

Because Clément's promotion to Major was not properly recorded, his **half-pay pension for life was calculated at the rank of Captain**, not Major. This was a significant financial loss for him and his widow.

Long after his death, **his children and heirs petitioned Congress** to correct this injustice.

In **1860**—44 years after Clément's death—the **Congressional Committee on Revolutionary Claims** examined the evidence, including:
- Testimony from **Jean Monty**, the regimental fifer who had served with Clément
- Military records
- Documentation of his service

The committee concluded that **Clément Gosselin had indeed been wronged**. They ordered that the difference in pay be granted to his heirs.

This 1860 decision came on the eve of the Civil War—a new generation fighting for their conception of American ideals, just as Clément had fought for his.

### Patriot Ancestor Recognition

In modern times, genealogical societies have recognized Clément's service:

**DAR (Daughters of the American Revolution)**:
- Patriot Ancestor A046437
- Verified service in the Continental Army

**SAR (Sons of the American Revolution)**:
- Patriot Ancestor P-168422
- Documented Revolutionary War service

This recognition means his descendants can join these organizations based on his service.

### October 2022: Beekmantown Monument

For over 200 years, Clément Gosselin's grave in **East Beekmantown Cemetery** remained unmarked.

On **October 8, 2022**, the **Town of Beekmantown** unveiled a monument to honor Major Clément Gosselin at the cemetery where he is buried.

The ceremony included:
- **John C. Gosselin**, a direct descendant, representing the Major in the Society of the Cincinnati
- Town Supervisor Norm Davis
- U.S. Congresswoman Elise Stefanik (or her representative)
- Representatives from:
  - **L'Ordre de Lafayette de Québec** (who initiated the project in 2013)
  - **L'Association des Familles Gosselin**
  - **L'Association de France-Québec**
  - Jean-Pierre Turcotte, Mayor of Ste-Famille (Clément's birthplace on Île d'Orléans)
  - The Pennsylvania Society of the Cincinnati

The monument's inscription honors:
- His birth on Île d'Orléans near Quebec City
- His service in the 2nd Canadian Regiment
- His work as a spy reporting directly to General George Washington
- His wound at the Battle of Yorktown
- His role in securing American independence

This monument represents international collaboration between American and Quebec organizations to honor a man who belonged to both worlds.

### October 2024: Quebec Government Recognition

In a remarkable development, in **October 2024**, the **Quebec government designated Clément Gosselin as a historic figure**.

This represents a significant shift:
- Acknowledgment of Quebec's complex relationship with the American Revolution
- Recognition that not all French-Canadians supported British rule
- Honoring those who made difficult choices and paid terrible prices

Approaching the **250th anniversary of the American invasion of Quebec** (1775-2025), there's renewed interest in this forgotten chapter of both American and Canadian history.

### Why He Matters

Clément Gosselin's story matters because:

**He represents complexity**: Not all Americans supported independence. Not all British subjects opposed it. French-Canadians were divided. History is messy.

**He embodies sacrifice**: He lost everything for a cause. Not for personal gain. Not because it was easy. Because he believed in liberty.

**He connects nations**: His story links American and Canadian history in ways often forgotten. The Revolution wasn't just 13 colonies—it was a North American conflict.

**He was a spy**: Intelligence work was crucial to the Revolution. Washington's spy networks are famous (the Culper Ring, etc.). Clément's Quebec network deserves similar recognition.

**He bridges cultures**: A French-speaking Catholic fighting for a Protestant, English-dominated rebellion. An ancestor who connects French-Canadian and American heritage.

In an era when we think of the American Revolution as a simple story—13 colonies vs. Britain—Clément Gosselin reminds us of the complexity:

**It was international**: French-Canadians, Native Americans, French forces, British troops, Hessian mercenaries—this was a North American and even global conflict.

## Historical Context: Quebec and the American Revolution

### The Invasion of Quebec (1775-1776)

The Continental Congress's attempt to bring Quebec into the Revolution was based on several assumptions:
- French-Canadians would resent British rule
- They would welcome American "liberation"
- Quebec could be the 14th colony

The reality was more complex:
- The Quebec Act (1774) had actually granted significant concessions to French-Canadians
- The Catholic Church opposed Protestant American rebels
- Many French-Canadians were wary of American intentions
- British military strength was significant

**The Campaign**:
- September 1775: Benedict Arnold begins march through Maine
- November 1775: Richard Montgomery captures Montreal
- December 31, 1775: Failed assault on Quebec City
- May 1776: American forces retreat completely from Canada

**Casualties**:
- Hundreds of Americans killed, wounded, or captured
- About 5,000 Americans died of smallpox during the campaign
- Relatively light British/Canadian losses

**Result**:
- Complete failure
- Quebec remained British
- Border between Canada and United States established

### The 2nd Canadian Regiment

Formed in early 1776 from French-Canadian volunteers, the 2nd Canadian Regiment served throughout the war:
- Fought in major battles (Saratoga, Yorktown, etc.)
- Provided intelligence from Quebec
- About 450-500 men at peak strength
- Commanded by Moses Hazen (controversial but effective)
- Many members settled in New York and Vermont after the war

The regiment was unique—Catholic, French-speaking, fighting for American independence but unable to ever return home.

### French-Canadian Loyalties

French-Canadians were divided:
- **Some supported the Americans**: Like Clément, seeing opportunity for liberty
- **Some supported the British**: Loyalists who had taken oaths or benefited from British rule
- **Most remained neutral**: Trying to survive and protect their families

The division created lasting rifts in Quebec society.

### Intelligence Operations

Washington relied heavily on spy networks:
- The Culper Ring in New York
- Various agents throughout British-held territory
- Clément's network in Quebec

Intelligence gathering was dangerous, sophisticated work requiring:
- Codes and ciphers
- Dead drops and secret communications
- Network management
- Constant risk of capture and execution

Clément's two years of spying (1778-1780) represent significant, underappreciated intelligence work.
